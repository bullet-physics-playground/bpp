## 4.1 Perfect formations (split Newtonian + (m, n) ordering attributes)
§4.1, PDF 124–130 (Figs 4.1–4.5, Listing 4.1 p.129).

**What it shows.** Every particle has a preferred slot in the lattice. Particles "dance" around each other until their ordering constraints hold, then the lattice relaxes into a *perfect* square or triangular formation with no defects. You can toggle square ↔ triangle at run time, and the whole formation tears down and rebuilds (Fig 4.4). "One is Born" clones a particle with fresh attributes, and the new particle ripples through the formation to its slot. The Desired_Separation slider can expand or contract the formation live. Text: "the process is incredibly robust."

**Agents.** Point particles. The GUI in Fig 4.3 shows 100 particles (Number_of_Particles slider at max = 100). Fig 4.1 illustrates the numbering with 18 particles.

**Identity → (ring, index) → (m, n).**
- `who` ids are converted to a ring number and an index within the ring (Fig 4.1).
- Rings for the triangular lattice: ring 0 has 1 particle, ring 1 has 6, ring 2 has 12 (in general 6k).
- Index 0 is always at the top-left of the ring.
- (ring, index) is then converted to (m, n) (Fig 4.2): if m_A < m_B, A should be left of B; if n_A < n_B, A should be below B.
- Each particle holds two (m, n) sets, one for the triangular lattice and one for the square lattice. The code is procedure `init-m-n`, which the text does not reproduce. Porter must derive the mapping, e.g. from Fig 4.2.
- Needs a shared frame (a compass for "left/below") and the exchange of 2 bytes (m, n) per neighbour over RF. 256 values per attribute.

**Force law (Listing 4.1, PDF 129).**
- Split Newtonian: F = G·m_i·m_j / r^p, capped at FMAX, applied within r < view·D.
- If r > D: attract (`attract 1 1`).
- If r < D, the pair normally repels, with these exceptions. They are evaluated in order; pm,pn = own attributes, km,kn = neighbour's, deltax/deltay = offset to neighbour:
  1. Ordering wrong on both axes (deltax<0 & pm<km & deltay<0 & pn<kn, or the mirror case) → `attract 2 2`.
  2. Ordering wrong in x (deltax<0 & pm<km, or deltax>0 & pm>km) → `attract 2 2`.
  3. Ordering wrong in y (same pattern with deltay, pn, kn) → `attract 2 2`.
  4. Same row (pn = kn) but |deltay| > cy·D, or same column (pm = km) but |deltax| > cx·D → `attract 2 2`.
  5. Triangle only, special case: pn ≠ kn and |deltay| < cy·D → `repulse 0 3`.
  6. Triangle only, special case: pm ≠ km and |deltax| < cx·D → `repulse 3 0`.
  7. Otherwise → `repulse 1 1`.
- Ambiguous; the porter must decide:
  - The meaning of the two arguments of `attract`/`repulse`. They are most likely per-axis multipliers on the x and y force components (e.g. `repulse 0 3` = pure y repulsion ×3). This is consistent with the text saying the special-case forces are not along the line joining the particles (Fig 4.5).
  - The values of `view`, `cx` and `cy`. Not given. Ch.3 used view = 1.5; cy is plausibly √3/2 and cx 1/2 for the triangle.
  - The sign convention of deltax.
- GUI defaults (Fig 4.3, zoomed):
  - G = 1000, p = 2.5, FMAX = 1.0
  - Friction = 0.50, Time_Step = 1.00, D (Desired_Separation) = 25
  - The "Good Gravitational Constant" monitor (from Eq. 3.4) reads 902.11.

**Breaking Newton's 3rd law (PDF 128–130).**
- In the triangle special cases the forces are equal and opposite but not along the line of centres (Fig 4.5).
- Linear momentum is conserved for both lattices.
- Angular momentum is conserved (≈0, e.g. −3.73E−13 in Fig 4.3) only for the square lattice. It oscillates while the triangle self-organises.
- This is deliberate: the triangle forms faster because the injected angular momentum lets particles revolve around each other.

**Results to reproduce (qualitative; no numbers given):**
- The square lattice forms perfectly.
- Toggling square ↔ triangle rebuilds the formation.
- The angular momentum trace stays ≈0 for the square and oscillates for the triangle; linear momentum stays ≈0 for both.
- A clone added with One is Born migrates to its slot.
- Changing D live rescales the lattice.

**Open questions posed by the author (PDF 130).** These are ready-made experiments:
- (a) Can perfect triangles form without breaking Newton's 3rd law?
- (b) Assign ids *after* the initial placement to reduce motion.
- (c) Swap (m, n) attributes by communication instead of moving particles.

**BPP conversion notes.**
1. 2D particle replica first, effort medium. Port Listing 4.1 using the existing split-Newtonian code plus a per-particle (m, n) table. Plot linear and angular momentum to reproduce the square-vs-triangle momentum signature. Add "toggle formation", "one is born" and live-D controls.
2. Then quadcopters at fixed altitude, flown with the existing 5 Hz waypoint scheme.
   - The constraint-driven "dance" means close passes inside D. Drones need a hard collision floor, or a velocity cap during the dance.
   - Expect the Ch.13 jitter issue with the Newtonian law. Friction helps.
   - Square is the easier first target because it is momentum-clean.
3. ★ 3D perfect formation (this is the plan's "Perfect Formation 3D"; the text gives only 2D):
   - Extend to (m, n, l) with "above/below" from an altimeter or baro. A shared frame is natural for drones (compass + GPS/baro).
   - Lattices: simple cubic (extension of the square rules), and FCC/HCP layers of the triangular lattice (needs ring/shell numbering in 3D).
   - The triangle special cases generalise to off-axis repulsion, which breaks angular momentum in 3D too. Log L as a vector.
4. ★ The author's open questions (a)–(c) make good BPP batch experiments: time-to-perfect-formation vs. method. Metric to define: the number of particles in their correct slot, or the RMS error to the ideal lattice.
5. The (m, n) → slot mapping is the main piece of groundwork. An optional variant is to compute it from the ideal lattice coordinates instead of reverse-engineering `init-m-n`.

## 4.2 Chain formations (elliptical force law)
§4.2, PDF 131–138 (Figs 4.6–4.14, Table 4.1, Listing 4.2 p.137). The full derivation and the hardware version are in Ch.12.

**What it shows.**
- An unorganised cluster self-organises into a long chain that keeps communication connectivity. Motivation: sewers, ventilation ducts, caves.
- The chain first collapses into a short line, then slowly expands (Fig 4.10).
- The centre of mass moves, because the law breaks Newton's 3rd law. But some robots stay near the original centre of mass (the deployment point) "extremely often."
- Chains curve. Clockwise curvature ↔ negative angular momentum, CCW ↔ positive (Figs 4.10, 4.11).

**Agents.** Point particles with a heading. 40 particles by default (Fig 4.9). A 100-particle run is shown in Fig 4.14.

**Force law (Listing 4.2).**
- D *equals* the sensor range (no 1.5·D zone here).
- For a neighbour with r < D: F = G·m²/r^p, capped at FMAX.
- θ = atan(deltay, deltax) is the bearing to the neighbour; c1 = sin(heading + θ), s1 = cos(heading + θ).
- dradius = sqrt(1 / (c1²/a2 + s1²/b2)), where a2 and b2 come from Major_Axis and Minor_Axis (presumably a² and b²; check Ch.12).
- If r > dradius: attract along the line of centres. Else: repel.
- So a particle is repelled from neighbours ahead of it or behind it (inside a long ellipse aligned with its heading) and attracted to neighbours at its sides.
- Heading changes toward or away from the neighbour (Fig 4.8), so the forces are not equal and opposite. Both linear and angular momentum are violated.

**GUI defaults (Fig 4.9, zoomed).**
- N = 40, Major_Axis = 1000, Minor_Axis = 1.0
- Power p = 2.0, Force_Maximum = 1.00, Friction = 0.50, Time_Step = 0.25
- Desired_Separation (= sensor range) = 15, Obstacle_Force = 20
- G is not on the GUI. Assume it is fixed or derived, as in Ch.3.
- Buttons: One is Born, Kill One. Monitor: Max Distance (the largest pairwise separation, used as a straightness measure).

**World.** Open arena (NetLogo). Obstacles are added by left-click; their detection distance is set by Obstacle_Force.

**Results to reproduce.**

Table 4.1 (PDF 135): Max Distance averaged over 50 runs, Major_Axis = 1000.

| Minor_Axis | Average Max Distance |
|---|---|
| 1 | 561 |
| 2 | 498 |
| 3 | 427 |
| 4 | 379 |
| 5 | 350 |

- For N = 40 and D = 15 a perfectly straight chain spans ≈ 39 × 15 = 585, so 561 means nearly straight. This sanity check is mine, not the book's.
- Fig 4.12: with `random-seed 1`, Minor = 1 gives a nearly straight chain and Minor = 5 a strongly curved one.
- Fig 4.13: with large obstacles the chain threads a route through them and bends minimally, like a stiff wire minimising its potential energy ("principle of least effort").
- Fig 4.14: 100 particles still form a chain; the algorithm scales.
- Known failures, stated in the text:
  - Deleting particles after formation opens gaps that do not close, so the chain is not robust to failure.
  - The final spacing is just out of sensor range.
  - Fix (Ch.12): use a simulated sensor range shorter than the real one.

**BPP conversion notes.**
1. 2D particle replica, effort easy–medium.
   - Needs a heading per particle plus heading update rules. The text does not spell them out beyond Fig 4.8: heading points along the net force. Porter must decide whether heading = direction of velocity.
   - Reproduce Table 4.1 with 50-run batches. This needs batch-run groundwork.
   - Add Max Distance and centre-of-mass drift metrics.
2. Obstacles: reuse the Ch.3 obstacle force. A ★ maze/tunnel world (corridor meshes in BPP) bridges to Ch.12.
3. Vehicles:
   - Ground robots (wheeled), which fits the sewer/cave story.
   - Quadcopters threading a building or culvert, at 1 altitude.
4. ★ 3D chain: the ellipse becomes an ellipsoid (prolate spheroid along the heading). The repulsion zone is fore/aft and attraction is lateral and vertical. Gives a 3D "rope" of drones through vertical shafts or caves.
   - Ambiguity: in 3D the attractive belt is a full torus, so expect the chain to coil. May need a vertical bias or an altitude band.
   - The plan's "chains 3D (Ch.12)" should start from this Ch.4 law plus the Ch.12 fixes (shorter sim range, gap closing).
5. Report angular momentum vs. curvature sign as a check (Fig 4.11).

## 4.3 Uniform coverage
§4.3, PDF 138–147 (Figs 4.15–4.20, Tables 4.2, Listings 4.3–4.4). Theory in Ch.11.

### 4.3a Biomimetic baseline ("termite" random turn on blockage), `uniform.nlogo`
PDF 139–143.

**Agents and world.**
- Point particles with heading, unit step.
- 84 × 84 world with a yellow wall of width 1 (non-toroidal).
- Divided into 9 equal cells: 4 corner cells (blue), 4 edge cells (green), 1 centre cell (red).
- Each cell counts visits.

**Algorithm (Listing 4.3).**
- Each tick, check whether the way is blocked: wall at patch-ahead 1, wall at patch-left-and-ahead 45° 2, wall at patch-right-and-ahead 45° 2, or any other particle in-cone 3 30°.
- While blocked and tries < 10: heading = random 360, tries + 1.
- If tries < 10, move fd 1; otherwise stay put.
- Then increment the current cell's counter.
- Only needs a forward-looking obstacle sensor; it does not distinguish wall from robot.
- Contrast with Gage's diffuse reflection, which needs the wall's orientation and a 0–180° turn.

**Metric (Eq. 4.1).**
- Deviation from uniformity = sqrt(Σ_i (P_i − 1/C)²), C = 9.
- Initial value sqrt((C−1)/C) = sqrt(8/9) = 0.9428090416 (only one cell visited).

**Results to reproduce.**
- Fig 4.16: a single particle's path (pen down) covers the whole area, including the periphery, at 2,000 / 12,500 / 37,000 / 89,000 steps.
- Fig 4.17: with 1 particle, corner cells are visited most, edge cells next, the centre least (ideal share 1/9 ≈ 0.111 each).
- Table 4.2: deviation from uniformity vs. N (long runs; exact length not stated).

| N | Deviation |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.027 |
| 10 | 0.026 |
| 100 | 0.016 |
| 1000 | 0.004 |
| 10000 | 0.003 |

0.003 is adopted as the "high quality" target.

### 4.3b Mean-free-path (physicomimetic) coverage, `uniform_mfpl.nlogo`
PDF 144–147.

**Algorithm (Listing 4.4).**
- Same as 4.3a, plus a `steps` counter.
- If steps = Mean_Free_Path_Length, make a random turn even if not blocked (a "virtual collision"). Reset steps on any turn.

**Theory.**
- Best MFP ≲ 0.65 × cell size (derived in Ch.11).
- Effective area is 80 × 80 (wall of width 1, plus robots stopping 1 unit before it). Cell = 26⅔, so the bound is 17⅓.
- The GUI monitor "Theoretical Mean Free Path Length" shows 17.33. The default MFP slider is 15.

**Experiments (Fig 4.19, PDF 146).**
- MFP swept 1..120. The cap is 120 because the longest path is 80√2 ≈ 113.
- Run A: 1 particle × 200,000,000 steps per MFP value.
- Run B: 100 particles × 2,000,000 steps.

**Results to reproduce (read from Fig 4.19).**
- Deviation falls below 0.003 for MFP ≲ 15. This is consistent with the 17⅓ bound, marked by a short black line.
- At large MFP the curves plateau: ≈0.027 for 1 particle and ≈0.017 for 100 particles, matching Table 4.2.
- 1-particle curve:
  - rises to a peak ≈0.024 at MFP ≈ 50;
  - drops sharply to ≈0.002 in the window MFP 53–65;
  - climbs back to ≈0.027 by 100–120.
- Explanation (Fig 4.20): 53⅓ is the distance from the left wall to the right edge of the centre cell; 66⅔ is the same edge reached from the upper-left corner.
- Footnote 5 says the 100-particle run also improves for MFP 40–55. In the figure, the green curve has a small bump of ≈0.005 near MFP 40, then falls to ≈0 around 55–60 before rising to its plateau.
- Fig 4.19 shows the 100-particle curve generally lower than the 1-particle curve, except in that window.
- General rule given in the text: use MFP ≈ 0.65 × cell size.

**BPP conversion notes (coverage already exists as particles per the plan; check it against these numbers).**
1. Verify the existing particle port reproduces Table 4.2, Fig 4.19 (including the 53–65 dip) and the corner > edge > centre ordering with no MFP.
   - Use the same 84 × 84 world, wall width 1, 9 cells, a cone of 3 / 30° for robot detection, and ±45° look-ahead at distance 2.
   - 200 M steps is heavy. Run headless batches with a fixed RNG seed.
2. Vehicles:
   - Ground robot with a bumper/IR forward sensor (matches Ch.11 hardware).
   - Quadcopter at fixed altitude with a forward range sensor. The "random turn" becomes a new heading; the drone flies at constant speed and turns on obstacle or when the path length reaches the MFP.
3. ★ 3D coverage of a volume (plan: coverage 3D, Ch.11):
   - Random direction uniform on the sphere.
   - Cells become 27 sub-cubes, so corner/edge/face/centre gives 4 visitation classes.
   - Re-derive the MFP bound for cubes; Ch.11 has the 2D derivation. Expect geometry-specific dips analogous to 53⅓ / 66⅔.
4. Ambiguous: the number of steps used for Table 4.2 is not given. Use "until steady."

## 4.4 Couette flow (kinetic theory, walls moving in opposite directions), `kt.nlogo`
§4.4, PDF 147–156 (Figs 4.21–4.24, Listing 4.5 p.151, Eqs 4.2–4.6). The full version is in Ch.7, where both walls move the same way and there are obstacles.

**What it shows.**
- A stochastic, non-F = ma swarm sweep down a vertical corridor.
- The left (yellow) wall moves down at Wall_Velocity; the right (red) wall moves up at Wall_Velocity.
- Result: the left half of the corridor sweeps down and the right half sweeps up.
- Momentum appears first at the walls and diffuses inward through collisions (Fig 4.22, after 15,032 steps).

**Agents.**
- 1000 particles by default (Fig 4.21).
- The text says 30 particles perform "almost identically, in the aggregate" (PDF 156).

**Algorithm (Listing 4.5).** Particles carry (vx, vy) and heading = atan(vx, vy). The position update is not shown; presumably fd by the speed each tick.
1. Virtual collision:
   - `friends` = other turtles in-radius 2. If any, pick one at random.
   - rel_speed = |v − v_f|; v_cm = ½(v + v_f).
   - θ = random 360 (integer degrees); v_rel = rel_speed·(sin θ, cos θ).
   - v = v_cm + ½·v_rel; v_f = v_cm − ½·v_rel.
   - This conserves linear momentum and relative speed (energy).
2. Wall thermalisation, when any wall patch is within radius 1:
   - Red (right) wall: vx = −sqrt(2T)·sqrt(−ln U) (Rayleigh, pointing away from the wall); vy = N(0,1)·sqrt(T) + Wall_Velocity.
   - Yellow (left) wall: vx = +sqrt(2T)·sqrt(−ln U); vy = N(0,1)·sqrt(T) − Wall_Velocity.
- k = m = 1.

**GUI defaults (Fig 4.21, zoomed).**
- N = 1000, Wall_Velocity = 1.0, Temperature = 0.010.
- Monitors: linear momentum x and y (should stay ≈0), Slope, Correlation Coefficient. A Resample button clears the statistics.
- Histogram: corridor split into 13 vertical columns, showing the mean vy per column.

**Results to reproduce.**
1. **Linear velocity profile.**
   - The theoretical slope across the 13 bars is 2·V_wall / 12, i.e. 1/6 ≈ 0.1666 at V = 1.
   - Experiment (Fig 4.23): V = 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0; 10,000 steps each, Resample at 5,000.
   - Measured slope ≈ theory, with only a slight deviation at V = 2.0. Correlation coefficient > 0.999 always. Steady runs reach ≈ 0.999.
2. **Mean speed vs. temperature, walls stationary.**
   - 2D theory: v̄ = ¼·sqrt(8πkT/m) = sqrt(π/2)·sqrt(T) ≈ 1.2533·√T (Eq. 4.5). This equals the mean of the Rayleigh wall distribution with s = √T.
   - 3D values for comparison: v̄ ≈ 1.5958√T (with variance), upper bound √3·√T ≈ 1.732√T.
   - Experiment (Fig 4.24): √T = 0.01..0.20 in steps of 0.01; 100,000 steps each, Resample at 50,000.
   - Fit: v̄ = 1.2617·√T + 0.0013, r = 0.9996 (Eq. 4.6).
3. Linear momentum stays ≈ 0. This follows from symmetric thermal noise, opposite wall velocities, and momentum-conserving collisions.

**Robot-implementation notes in the text.**
- A robot needs the wall orientation (ring of IR or sonar).
- A digital compass gives a common frame, and (vx, vy) are exchanged over the RF trilateration link for the v_cm computation.
- Any wall in the "Couette direction" is treated as a Couette wall, even an obstacle (§4.5).
- Ch.7 compares this approach with a pheromone biomimetic sweep: nearly as good coverage in one-third of the time, and harder to deceive.

**BPP conversion notes.**
1. 2D particle replica, effort easy.
   - Corridor with two moving walls (kinematic wall bodies in BPP, or a purely virtual wall velocity). The wall need not physically move; only the thermal boundary condition uses V.
   - Implement the collision step as velocity reassignment, not as contact physics: it is a virtual collision at range 2.
   - Reproduce Fig 4.23 (slope = V/6, r > 0.999) and Fig 4.24 (1.2617 vs. 1.2533). These are excellent automated regression tests.
   - Groundwork: the corridor/column histogram, a Resample (burn-in) step, batch sweeps.
   - Ambiguity: the corridor width and length are not given. The histogram x-axis in Fig 4.21 runs to 780, unclear. Choose a width so that 13 columns of ≥ 2 units each fit.
2. Vehicles:
   - Commanded velocities need velocity-command groundwork, as opposed to waypoints.
   - Ground robots (Ch.7 hardware-style).
   - Quadcopters at one altitude, where velocity jumps are smoothed by the controller. Check that the profile survives the motor lag.
   - A point-mass-with-limits version first, with the speed cap and acceleration limit as parameters.
3. ★ 3D Couette: a slab between two parallel planes moving in opposite directions, swept by drones.
   - Wall thermalisation in 3D: Rayleigh-type normal component, Gaussian tangential components plus the wall velocity.
   - Collisions: v_rel direction uniform on the sphere.
   - Prediction: the profile stays linear, and the 3D Maxwell mean speed is 1.5958√T (the formula the book gives for 3D).
   - Also a cylindrical pipe-flow variant (Poiseuille with a body force), the analogue of Ch.7's same-direction walls.
4. This chapter is the minimal precursor of the plan's "kinetic theory 3D (Ch.7)". Validate here first.

## 4.5 Summary (PDF 156–157)
No new experiment.
- Restates the requirements: local neighbours, a compass, a few bytes of communication.
- Coverage needs only obstacle detection, without wall/robot discrimination.
- Sweeping needs only the global direction of the Couette wall.

## Additions / contradictions to the existing plan
- **Perfect Formation 3D (Ch.4)**:
  - The text is 2D only.
  - The triangular case deliberately breaks Newton's 3rd law (angular momentum not conserved). Expect this to interact badly with the drone controller. Do the square/cubic version first.
  - The three open questions on PDF 130 should be added as experiments.
  - The (m, n) mapping code (`init-m-n`) is not printed and must be reconstructed.
- **Chains 3D (Ch.12)**: Ch.4 has the base elliptical law and the Table 4.1 benchmark, which the plan does not mention. The 2D chain with obstacles (Fig 4.13) should be ported before Ch.12's maze and hardware version.
- **Uniform coverage (already done as particles)**: the plan should add verification against Table 4.2, Fig 4.19 (including the 53–65 MFP anomaly) and the 9-cell 84 × 84 geometry. The plan has the mean-free-path version but not the no-MFP biomimetic baseline or the N-sweep.
- **Couette flow (opposite walls)** is a new entry, not in the plan. It is cheap, has two closed-form checks (slope V/6; v̄ = 1.2533√T), and is the stepping stone to "kinetic theory 3D (Ch.7)".
- Groundwork this chapter requires that is not yet listed:
  - heading-dependent (anisotropic) force laws;
  - velocity-command interface (kinetic theory);
  - per-cell visit counters and histograms;
  - batch runs with seeds (50-run averages; sweeps of 1..120);
  - momentum (linear and angular) logging as a correctness check.
