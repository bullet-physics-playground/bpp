# Chapter 12 — Chain Formations

- **Author:** Paul M. Maxim (portions from Hettiarachchi, Maxim, W. Spears, D. Spears, ICCAS 2008)
- **PDF pages:** 398–443 (book pp. 367–412; book page = PDF page − 31)
- **Cross-ref:** Chap. 4 has a short intro to the same material (elliptical disk, Figs. 4.6, 4.7). Chap. 10 covers the Maxelbot and its trilateration/RF localization. Chap. 11 sets the units (1 pixel = 1 inch, 1 step = 1 s).

**Task:** A stationary robot 0 sits at the entrance of an unknown, long, narrow environment (sewer, duct, tunnel, cave). More robots are released one at a time in front of it. They self-organise into a connected chain that follows the passage. Every robot keeps line of sight with its upstream neighbour. The chain also carries a relay of positions and headings back to robot 0, so the user gets a map. The hard part is staying connected around corners: robots pause at a corner, should pop out once enough robots queue behind, and should reconnect after a break. Corners get no special-case code (p. 399).

**Units used throughout the simulation (p. 403):** 1 px = 1 inch and 1 time step = 1 s. A Maxelbot moves 1 ft/s, so max speed must be ≤ 12. The value used is 3.

## E1. Elliptical split-Newtonian force law (the core modification)
**Section 12.2, pp. 399–401 (book 368–370), Fig. 12.2. NetLogo model: "formation chain.nlogo".**

- **What it shows:** The circular repulsion disk of the split Newtonian law becomes an ellipse whose major axis lies along the robot's heading. Robot p is repelled by any robot k inside p's ellipse. It is attracted to any robot outside the ellipse but closer than **1.5D**. Two robots heading straight at each other repel. Two robots at other relative angles attract, turn toward each other, and then repel. With many robots the result is an emergent chain.
- **Math (in p's local frame, x = forward):**
  - Ellipse: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1 (12.1), where a is the semi-major axis and b the semi-minor axis.
  - Distance to the ellipse along bearing θ: **d = 1 / sqrt(cos²θ/a² + sin²θ/b²)** (from Eq. 12.2).
  - Sign of the force (12.3): **repulsive if r < d, attractive if r ≥ d**. Here r = |r| is the sensed range from trilateration.
  - Magnitude: the Ch. 3 split Newtonian law F = G·m_i·m_j/r^p, with the EA-tuned robot–robot G and p (E4).
- **Agents:** point robots with a heading. They turn on the spot ("turn on a dime") and have a max speed.
- **Results to reproduce:** The NetLogo demo shows two robots alternating between attraction and repulsion, and many robots forming chains.
- **BPP notes:** Add a 2D particle replica first. Particles must carry a heading, because the force sign depends on the bearing relative to heading. Heading = direction of the last velocity is fine for particles. Effort: easy.
  - ★ **3D:** The natural generalisation is a prolate ellipsoid along the heading, x²/a² + (y²+z²)/b² = 1, so d = 1/sqrt(cos²θ/a² + sin²θ/b²) with θ the angle off the heading axis. This gives chains in 3D tunnels and pipes (drones in a culvert or mine shaft).
  - **Porter must decide how big the ellipse really is.** See the note in E4: with the evolved axes, robot–robot attraction almost never happens inside sensing range.

## E2. Backflow force
**Section 12.3, pp. 401–402 (book 370–371).**

- **Mechanism:** When a robot loses connectivity with its closest upstream neighbour, it is pushed toward that neighbour's **last known location**. The force is applied only while the link is lost and switches off once the link is back. Without it, repelling robots drift out of range and stop interacting.
- **Effect when a robot dies:** The robot downstream of a dead robot reconnects to the next upstream robot. The chain gets shorter but stays connected.
- **Parameters (Listing 12.2):** back-flow force magnitude **0.371287**; back-flow friction **0.569767**. The backflow friction is a separate friction used while backflow is active, and its exact use is not spelled out.
- **Ambiguities:**
  - "Upstream" is not formally defined. The natural reading is the neighbour closer to robot 0 along the chain, e.g. by MST parent or by release order.
  - The text does not say whether the force has constant magnitude in the direction of the last known position. Constant magnitude is implied.

## E3. Front tangential force
**Section 12.4, p. 402, Listing 12.1 (C code).**

- If the front IR sensor (sensor index s == 0) detects a wall, add a force of magnitude `front_tangential_force` at heading ± π/2. Left or right is chosen 50/50 with U(0,1) > 0.5, and the choice is made on **every step the wall is detected** (the code shows no memory of the previous choice).
- **Code convention:** wall_force.x += F·sin(heading ± π/2) and wall_force.y += F·cos(heading ± π/2). Heading is therefore compass-style: 0 = +y (north), clockwise positive.
- **Parameter:** front tangential force magnitude **2.082259** (Listing 12.2).
- **Purpose:** helps the chain expand around corners.

## E4. EA parameter optimisation
**Section 12.5, pp. 402–404, Listing 12.2.**

- **Algorithm:** A maximising EA over real-valued vectors, with mutation and recombination; only the best individuals produce the next generation. Fitness = (reachability + sparseness + coverage)/3 (Eq. 12.7), averaged over time, evaluated in the environment of Fig. 12.3. Population size, generations and operator rates are **not given**.
- **Fixed parameters (7):**
  - N = 10 robots
  - desired distance D = 40
  - max speed = 3
  - 3 IR sensors at 0°, 90°, 270°
  - motor noise 10%
  - one robot added every **2,000** steps
  - total 100,000 steps
- **Evolved parameters (11), Listing 12.2:**

  | # | Parameter | Value |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1 | Friction of the system | 0.579306 |
  | 2 | Wall detection range | 7.381481 |
  | 3 | Ellipse major axis | 119,382.723558 |
  | 4 | Ellipse minor axis | 95.740130 |
  | 5 | Back-flow force magnitude | 0.371287 |
  | 6 | Back-flow friction | 0.569767 |
  | 7 | Robot–robot G | 1,895.527010 |
  | 8 | Robot–robot p (distance power) | 3.022846 |
  | 9 | Robot–wall G | 1,218.359939 |
  | 10 | Robot–wall p | 5.832936 |
  | 11 | Front tangential force magnitude | 2.082259 |

- **Porter observations (my analysis, not stated in the book):**
  - (a) The major axis is effectively infinite. Then d ≈ b/|sinθ|, so a robot k is repelled whenever its **lateral offset |r·sinθ| < b**. With b = 95.7, or 47.9 if "axis" means the full axis, and a sensing range of 1.5D = 60, nearly every in-range neighbour is **repulsive**. Cohesion therefore comes almost entirely from backflow. This matches E11: robots are slowly pushed out and backflow pulses them back.
  - (b) The steep law F ∝ 1/r^3.02 gives tiny forces at r = 40: 1895.5/40^3.02 ≈ 0.028. Wall force at 7 px is ≈ 0.014. These forces only matter close in.
  - (c) Friction 0.579 is presumably the Ch. 3 velocity-damping coefficient. Whether it is applied as v ← v·(1−μ) or v ← v·μ is ambiguous; check against the Ch. 3 code convention.
  - (d) Wall range 7.38 px is about 7.4 inches. On hardware they used 9 inches, "slightly greater than the value found by the EA" (p. 437), which confirms 1 px = 1 inch.
- **BPP notes:** This is the reference parameter set. A port should first replay these values. Re-running the EA needs a batch, headless mode (see E5) and is medium to hard. ★ It could be re-evolved for 3D tunnels.

## E6. Performance metrics
**Section 12.6.2, pp. 410–412, Eqs. 12.4–12.7.**

- **Graph:** G = (V, E) with edges = communication links (line of sight and within 1.5D), weighted by length. G_c is the component connected to robot 0, with node set V_c and edge set E_c. G_MST is the MST of G_c.
- **Metrics:**
  - reachability = |V_c|/N (12.4)
  - sparseness = (|V_c|−1)/|E_c| (12.5)
  - coverage = Σ_{i=0}^{|V_c|−2} l_i^{G_MST} / (R_max·(N−1)) (12.6)
  - fitness = (reachability + sparseness + coverage)/3 (12.7)
- All range over [0, 1], with 1 optimal. **N = total robots placed.** Anything beyond the first unbroken chain counts as lost.
- **Ambiguity:** R_max is presumably 1.5D = 60, the maximum link length.
- **BPP notes:** These need a graph and MST computation per logged step (Prim's algorithm on a small N). Easy.

## E10. Experiment III — robustness (add, then randomly kill)
**Section 12.6.7, pp. 417–418, Fig. 12.14.**

- **Setup:**
  - noise 10%
  - start with 1 robot and add one every **500** steps until step 24,500 (50 robots)
  - run 500 more steps with 50 robots
  - then remove one **random** robot every 500 steps until 2 remain
  - 10 runs
- **Results (Fig. 12.14, x-axis N going up to 50 and back down to 2):**
  - **Reachability** climbs to about 0.88–0.9 by N = 10–50, peaks near 0.9 at N = 50, then declines to about 0.8 as robots die.
  - **Coverage** jumps to about 0.6 early, then drifts to about 0.5.
  - **Sparseness** is about 0.63 early, dips to about 0.52 at N = 50, and rises back to about 0.62.
  - **Fitness** is remarkably flat at about 0.68–0.70, ending around 0.63.
  - A sparseness of about 0.5 means redundant links, which compensate for failures.

## E11. Backflow force analysis
**Section 12.6.8, p. 419.**

- **Setup:** Only 2 robots in a straight corridor, 4,000 steps.
- **Results to reproduce:**
  - Backflow fires as **single one-step pulses**, on average **every 35 steps**, so a robot is in range 34/35 of the time.

## E12. Ideal-chain analysis (closed form)
**Section 12.6.9, pp. 419–421, Eqs. 12.8–12.10.**

- **Model:** Each link is up with probability p, independently. L is the chain length from robot 0, ranging over 0..N−1.
  - P(l) = p^l(1−p) for l < N−1, and P(N−1) = p^(N−1).
  - **E[L] = (p − p^N)/(1 − p)** (12.10).
- **Book examples:**

  | Case | p | N | E[L] stated |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | target ≈ 75% | 0.99 | 50 | ≈ 37 |
  | backflow measurement | 34/35 ≈ 0.97 | 50 | 25.785 (51.6%) |
  | sparseness 0.5 (two links must fail) | 0.999 (from (1−p)² = (1/35)² ≈ 0.001) | 50 | 47.8 (95.6%) |

  - Checks: E[L] = 25.79 and 47.79 are correct. For p = 0.99, (0.99 − 0.99^50)/0.01 = **38.5**, not 37. This is a minor discrepancy in the book.
  - The book compares 95.6% with the maximum reachability achieved in Fig. 12.14.
- **BPP notes:** Monte-Carlo check of the formula against simulated link uptime. Trivial.

## E13. Distributed chain-formation list (CFL) protocol
**Section 12.7.1, pp. 422–426, Fig. 12.15, Listing 12.5.**

- **Purpose:** Build a chain-ordered list of the robots in Θ(N) by piggybacking on the RF token of the Chap. 10 localization protocol. RF reaches everyone; acoustic signals need not.
- **Data:**
  - robots[i] = [x, y, heading] of robot i in the receiver's local frame
  - CFL[] = ordered IDs
  - CFLinfo[] = [x, y, heading] of each CFL entry, in the frame of the **previous** CFL entry
- **Algorithm:**
  - Pass 1 initialises. Each robot appends own ID + 1 by default, because it does not yet know its neighbours.
  - From pass 2 on, robot 0 resets the lists and appends its nearest robot.
  - Each robot whose ID was last appended appends its nearest robot not yet in the CFL, then broadcasts CFL[] and CFLinfo[].
  - The last robot rebroadcasts unchanged, and the token returns to robot 0.
- **Worked example (N = 4, Listing 12.5):** The result is CFL = [0, 3, 2, 1].
  - Data string: [0 3 2 1 | 0.0 0.0 0 | −14.0 4.0 270 | 15.0 16.0 180 | 16.0 20.0 90]
- **Rates and headings:** An RF transmission occurs about every **120 ms (≈ 8 Hz)**. Headings come from a compass (0° = north, clockwise). Alignment without a compass is possible at 2× the communication.
- **Alternative protocol (not implemented):** Broadcast only [ID1, ID2, x2, y2, h1] (sender, last added robot and its coordinates in the sender's frame, sender heading). Every robot rebuilds the CFL itself.
- **BPP notes:**
  - Implement it as a message-passing layer: token ring, per-hop packet loss, and relative frames with compass noise.
  - This gives the "map relayed back to base" display.
  - Effort: medium.
  - ★ For drones, the same nearest-unlisted-neighbour chain can be built over the radio model, which feeds the relay-chain idea (E17).

## E16. Protocol reliability analysis
**Section 12.7.4, p. 428.**

- If each hop succeeds with probability p, a full pass over N robots succeeds with probability p^N. With p = 0.9 and N = 5, that is **59%**. A lost token is regenerated by robot 0.
- In the hardware experiments, measured reachability is p_e = p^5, from which p is backed out.

## Summary of what Ch. 12 adds to the plan

- **The plan says only "chains 3D (Ch. 12)". That is too thin.** A faithful port needs these pieces, which the existing work lacks:
  1. wall-polygon worlds from vertex lists
  2. IR ray sensors
  3. line-of-sight-gated neighbour sensing within 1.5D
  4. robot heading in the force law (elliptical sign switch)
  5. the backflow memory (last known upstream position) and the front tangential force
  6. sequential release from a stationary base
  7. metrics with MST
  8. multiplicative noise
  9. batch/headless runs with 10 seeds
- **Recommended order:**
  1. 2D particle replica in the Listing 12.4 maze with the Listing 12.2 parameters.
  2. Check the backflow-period test (E11), then Experiments I–III.
  3. Port the hardware experiments with 5 robots.
  4. ★ 3D tunnel chains with quadcopters.
- **Contradiction with the plan's Newtonian jitter note:** The evolved chain law is essentially **always-repulsive plus a backflow pulse** (E4 note a), not a balanced attract/repel lattice. On drones this pulse-driven behaviour will probably jitter like the Newtonian law did. Ch. 13's adaptive and static friction (Eqs. 13.23 and 13.25) should be applied when chains are flown on quadcopters.
- **★ New 3D ideas this chapter invites:**
  - ellipsoidal repulsion volume along the heading
  - chains through vertical shafts or multi-level tunnels
  - the chain as a **comms relay** carrying the CFL map back to a base drone or ground station
  - branching at junctions as a feature
  - loops reconnecting
  - occupancy-trail maps built from ray hits
- **Release rate ambiguity:** The EA text says 2,000 steps; the configuration file and Experiment III say 500. The porter should use 500 for Experiment III and state the choice for Experiments I and II.
