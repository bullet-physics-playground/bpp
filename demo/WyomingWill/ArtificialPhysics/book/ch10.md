# Chapter 10 — What Is a Maxelbot?

- **Author:** Paul M. Maxim (Wyoming DOT; formerly Univ. of Wyoming)
- **PDF pages:** 332–371 (book pp. 301–339)
- **Nature of chapter:** hardware/electrical engineering. It describes the trilateration localization module, the XSRF ultrasonic range finder, the RF token-ring synchronization protocol, the Maxelbot robot (MMP-5 base) and its add-on sensors. It contains very few "simulations". What it does contain is the **sensor and vehicle model** that every later hardware chapter (11, 12, 14, 15 and the outdoor formation runs; check others) relies on. That makes it groundwork for BPP rather than a demo to reproduce.

## E10.1 Closed-form 2D trilateration (L-shaped receivers)
- **Section/pages:** §10.3–10.4, PDF 333–335 (Fig. 10.1, Eqs. 10.1–10.3).
- **What it shows:** robot 1 has three acoustic receivers (base points) A=(0,d), B=(0,0), C=(d,0) in its own egocentric frame. X points to the robot's front. Robot 2 emits an RF pulse and an acoustic pulse at the same time from its transducer B. Robot 1 measures the distances a, b, c (differential time of arrival: RF ≈ instantaneous, sound 343 m/s) and solves
  - `x2B = (b² − c² + d²) / (2d)`, `y2B = (b² − a² + d²) / (2d)`.
  - The general linear system, before the L-shape simplification, is given on PDF 335.
- **Parameters:** baseline **d = 15.24 cm (6 in)**. The text states that error falls as d grows and as the clock speed of the trilateration module rises (a faster clock shortens range because of counter size) [ref 88, Heil].
- **Properties:** fully distributed, with no global frame. It is one-to-many: every listening robot localizes the emitter in the same pulse.
- **Result to reproduce:** none numerically. The formula is checkable: under noise-free ranges it should return the exact position.
- **BPP notes:**
  - This is the core of a **"trilateration sensor" module** in Lua. Take true positions, compute a, b, c from the three receiver points on the listening body (rotated by its yaw), add range noise (see E10.4/E10.9), then apply the formula. This replaces the "perfect relative position" that AP currently uses.
  - Easy.
  - ★ **3D extension:** add a 4th receiver D=(0,0,d), giving `z = (b² − e² + d²)/(2d)`, where e is the range to D. On a quadcopter the four receivers would sit on three arm tips plus a mast. The noise sensitivity of z with a small vertical baseline is a nice study.
  - **Physical caveat for the porter:** the hardware works only in a horizontal plane (see E10.6), so a "Maxelbot-faithful" sensor should be 2D only.

## E10.2 Multi-hop coordinate sharing / relayed localization
- **Section/pages:** §10.4, PDF 335.
- **What it shows:** robot 1 cannot hear robot 3, but robot 2 can localize both 1 and 3. If 2 sends its measurements to 1, then 1 can compute 3's position and also the robots' orientations. Errors compound with path length; using multiple paths helps. There is no experiment; details are in Heil [88].
- **Data piggy-backing:** bytes appended to the RF pulse. A coordinate transform converts robot 2's frame into robot 1's frame.
- **BPP notes:**
  - Algorithm only. It becomes useful for sparse 3D swarms where a drone is out of acoustic/radio range.
  - Medium: it needs a message layer and frame transforms.
  - ★ Interesting experiment: plot localization error against hop count, with and without multi-path averaging.

## E10.3 Modified SRF04 range accuracy test
- **Section/pages:** §10.5.2, PDF 336–338, Fig. 10.3.
- **Setup:** the off-the-shelf SRF04 is modified so that the Rx transducer is removed and placed apart from Tx, giving a direct one-way distance measurement.
  - Tx and Rx are 30 cm above the floor.
  - One reading every 10 ms, with 130 readings per distance.
  - Distances run from 10 to 200 cm in 10 cm steps.
  - The specification range is 3 cm to 3 m; current draw is 30 mA; the device uses 40 kHz, an 8-cycle burst and a 10 µs trigger.
- **Result (Fig. 10.3):** accurate up to about 1 m. Near 2 m it tends to report slightly **short** distances, and scatter grows visibly beyond about 140 cm (readings spread roughly ±15–20 cm at 170–200 cm).
- **BPP notes:**
  - This is a range-noise calibration curve: bias ≈ 0 below 1 m, a slight negative bias and growing variance toward 2 m.
  - Optional; easy if a noise model is wanted.

## E10.4 Acoustic wavefront-detection jitter and its fixes (XSRF)
- **Section/pages:** §10.5.3–10.5.3.1, PDF 338–342, Fig. 10.6, Listing 10.1.
- **Model:** speed of sound 343 m/s at 20 °C, 40 kHz, so **λ = 8.58 mm**.
  - The received burst has an attack/decay envelope. Detection may fire on the 1st, 2nd or 3rd wavefront, so range errors are **quantized in multiples of 8.58 mm**.
- **Fixes:**
  - (a) The burst alternates between the normal signal and its complement. The text says this improves average range accuracy "by at most 4.25 mm".
  - (b) A second comparator on the positive half-wave detects half a wavelength sooner.
  - (c) Comparator thresholds are set to **+2.0 V / −2.0 V**, a compromise between missing the signal and false alarms.
  - (d) The transmit side generates the 8-cycle burst in 200 µs. Drive is boosted to about 16 V by a MAX232.
- **BPP notes:**
  - A realistic range-noise model is `range = true + k·λ/2 + gaussian`, where k is a small non-negative integer (missed half-wavelengths) and λ/2 ≈ 4.3 mm.
  - Low priority, but cheap to add.

## E10.5 XSRF amplification / range progression
- **Section/pages:** §10.5.3.2–10.5.3.3, PDF 342–344, Figs. 10.7–10.8.
- **Facts:**
  - Transmit/receive switching on a single transducer: the receiving transducer is used for both, through a MAX362 switch.
  - Two amplifier stages of gain 18 each give **324**, which yields a consistent range of only about **2 ft**.
  - A third stage brings gain to **5,832**, but noise becomes too high.
  - A bandpass filter (gain 18, centred on 40 kHz) gives accurate readings to **4 ft** on the breadboard prototype.
  - The PCB version reaches a useful range of **9–11 ft** [155].
- **Demos:**
  - `2robots.avi`: an autonomous Maxelbot follows an R/C-driven Maxelbot. The smoothness of the following reflects trilateration quality.
  - `3robots.avi`: three autonomous robots keep a formation outdoors.
- **BPP notes:**
  - **Sensor max range ≈ 3 m (10 ft); beyond that, accuracy collapses.** Ch. 11 confirms that accuracy "reduces drastically above 10 ft".
  - ★ Leader–follower demo: a Maxelbot follows a teleoperated or scripted leader using only trilateration, holding a set offset. The LCD example in E10.13 uses **26 in directly behind**. Easy once a ground robot exists.

## E10.6 Parabolic cones: channelling acoustic energy into a horizontal plane
- **Section/pages:** §10.5.4.1, PDF 345–346, Fig. 10.9.
- **Facts:**
  - The 400ST/R160 transducer has a main lobe of about **30°** at about 36 in. Covering 360° directly would take 12 transducers per base point, or 36 per robot.
  - Instead, each base point has one transducer pointing **down** at a parabolic cone, tip up. The cone's focal point is 1 in above its tip, and the optimal gap from transducer face to cone tip is **7/8 in** (the membrane sits about 1/8 in inside the face).
  - The cone reflects energy into and out of the **horizontal plane**.
- **BPP notes:**
  - The sensor is omnidirectional in azimuth but planar. In 3D, robots at different heights would not hear each other, or would hear them weakly.
  - A port needs to decide one of two things:
    - (a) Model a vertical "beam" limit, e.g. elevation |θ| < 15°, with signal loss outside it.
    - (b) Assume a 3D-capable sensor, which goes beyond the book.
  - This matters for any 3D drone version of Maxelbot sensing: ★ a "Maxelbot-faithful" 3D swarm would need to fly in layers, or carry a different sensor.

## E10.7 RF link and modified Manchester encoding (algorithm)
- **Section/pages:** §10.5.4.2–10.5.4.4, PDF 345–348.
- **Facts:**
  - The radio is an AM-RTD-315 transceiver: 315 MHz, half-duplex, up to 10 kbps.
  - The link runs at 9,600 Bd, which Manchester encoding halves to an effective **4,800 Bd**, so about **1.67 ms per byte**.
  - Modified encoding, with odd bits masked by 0xAA and even bits by 0x55: worked example 0x0E → `0101 1010 1010 0110`. Standard Manchester would give `0101 0101 1010 1001`.
  - Decoding: `(hi & 0xAA) | (lo & 0x55)`.
  - A stream that is not balanced is treated as a bit error and discarded.
- **BPP notes:** not physical. Model it as message latency (about 1.67 ms/byte) plus a packet-drop probability. Easy. Optional unit test of the encode/decode.

## E10.8 Trilateration module timing (RF + acoustic)
- **Section/pages:** §10.5.4.5–10.6, PDF 348–351, Figs. 10.12–10.13.
- **Facts:**
  - The controller is a Minidragon (25 MHz MC9S12DG256, 16-bit timers, input capture).
  - The module has 3 XSRF boards: two are receive-only and one switches between Tx and Rx.
  - A **250 µs delay** separates the trigger from the echo line being raised.
  - The echo-line widths for A, B and C are converted to distances.
- **BPP notes:** fold this into the trilateration sensor as a fixed processing latency (negligible). Easy.

## E10.9 Trilateration accuracy experiment (grid test)
- **Section/pages:** §10.6.1, PDF 351–352, **Fig. 10.14**.
- **Setup:** a robot is placed on the lab floor with transducer B at (0, 0). The text says the receivers are at (0,0), (0,6), (6,0) in.
  - A transmitter is placed at grid points from **(−24, −24) to (24, 24) in**, with a 12 in spacing visible in Fig. 10.14.
  - The text says "24 grid points"; the figure shows a 5×5 grid, i.e. 25 positions, with one sitting on the receivers.
- **Results to reproduce:**
  - Mean error **0.6 in**, minimum **0.1 in**, maximum **1.2 in** over the grid.
  - At **9–11 ft**, error is **at most 11%** [155].
- **BPP notes:**
  - This is the calibration target for the trilateration noise model. Choose a per-range σ so that a simulated version of this grid test reproduces mean 0.6 in and max 1.2 in within ±2 ft, and about 11% relative error at about 3 m.
  - Suggested model: σ_range ≈ a few mm near the robot, growing with distance. Also apply the cancellation: errors in a, b, c are amplified by about r/d in the solved position, which naturally gives growth with distance.
  - Easy; it doubles as a unit test of E10.1.

## E10.10 Token-ring synchronization protocol (with recovery)
- **Section/pages:** §10.7, PDF 352–355, Listing 10.2.
- **Algorithm:**
  - N robots have unique hardware IDs 0…N−1; N is known in advance. The robot whose ID equals `broadcast_ID` sends:
    1. sync byte **0x33** (`00110011`, not encoded)
    2. heading byte (from the compass), or another payload such as the chemical concentration ρ for Chs. 8–9
    3. own ID (which acts as the RF pulse)
    4. two FILL bytes (time for the receivers to prepare)
    5. LED blink
    6. acoustic pulse
    7. switch back to RX
  - All other robots wait for the sync byte, read heading and ID, and trilaterate.
  - Everyone then sets `broadcast_ID = (broadcast_ID + 1) mod N`.
- **Timing:**
  - Theoretical minimum between broadcasts: **10 ms**, to let echoes dissipate.
  - Actual: **120 ms per token pass**. The RF switch is specified at under 5 ms, but the data is unreliable until about 100 ms after switching, so fill bytes are sent for about 120 ms.
  - So each robot sees a given neighbour's position update every **120 ms × N**: about 0.84 s with N = 7, or about 1.2 Hz per neighbour. Pulses are 8.33 Hz in total.
- **Recovery:**
  - A software watchdog ISR increments a counter about every **88 ms**, reset before the first byte is received.
  - In the listing, robot 0 declares failure after `roll > 50`, and sets `broadcast_ID = N−1` so that it broadcasts next. The code comment says "11.36 is a 1 second delay", which is consistent with 88 ms ticks.
  - The text says the threshold is chosen based on N.
  - On a protocol failure, robots **stop moving**.
  - Tested by switching off robots at random; the text says the protocol "always recovered automatically".
- **Ambiguity for the porter:** as written, if robot k dies, the token stalls at k. Robot 0 restarts at 0, and the ring runs 0…k−1 again, so robots k+1…N−1 would never broadcast. The text claims full recovery but does not explain how this case is handled.
  - Decide: (a) use the listing literally and observe starvation; (b) have each robot skip a silent ID after a timeout. Option (b) is more robust.
- **BPP notes:**
  - This is the **sensing-rate model** that should replace AP's 5 Hz omniscient update in "hardware-faithful" runs: one emitter per 120 ms slot, everyone else updates that one neighbour.
  - Implications to check: Ch. 13's jitter story, and staleness of neighbour positions in fast drone swarms. ★ Investigate how large N can grow before the 120 ms × N staleness destabilizes formations at drone speeds.
  - Medium: it needs a per-robot neighbour table with timestamps and a scheduler.

## E10.11 Centralized vs modular robot architecture (behavioural consequence)
- **Section/pages:** §10.8.1, PDF 356–357, Fig. 10.15.
- **Facts:**
  - In the first, centralized design, the robot could compute and move only just before its own broadcast. Trilaterating robots block, so **"only one robot can move at a time"**.
  - The modular design moves localization onto its own Minidragon, which is the I²C master and pushes data to the second controller. Any robot can then move at any time.
  - Pins used on the HCS12: 14 of 112.
- **BPP notes:**
  - Optional. A "one robot moves per token slot" mode is an interesting, nearly free variant of the scheduler in E10.10. It is sequential (Gauss–Seidel-like) rather than simultaneous (Jacobi) updating, and it may suppress the Newtonian jitter.
  - ★ Worth a quick test against Ch. 13.

## E10.12 Localization module I/O formats
- **Section/pages:** §10.8.2–10.8.3, PDF 357–361, Listing 10.3.
- **I²C:**
  - 100 kbps, with pull-ups of 8 kΩ or less. The slave address is 1010 101.
  - Per robot the payload is {float x (4 B), float y (4 B), heading byte (0–255 ↦ 0–360°)}, i.e. 9 B, ordered by ID (27 B for 3 robots), followed by own_ID.
- **Serial:** 9600 8N1. Each line carries ID, x, y and heading.
- **BPP notes:**
  - The neighbour table in BPP should hold exactly (x, y, heading) per ID, with heading quantized to 1.41°.
  - Easy.

## E10.13 Maxelbot vehicle (MMP-5 base) and add-ons
- **Section/pages:** §10.8.4–10.9, PDF 361–370, Figs. 10.17–10.22.
- **Base platform (MMP-5, The Machine Lab):**
  - Mass 4.5 lb, payload 4 lb, 12 V 4.5 Ah NiMH battery.
  - Four wheels, one motor each with a high-torque gearhead, driven as left and right pairs. Tank (skid) steering with **zero turning radius**.
  - Command: PWM pulse of **1.0 ms (full reverse) – 1.5 ms (stop) – 2.0 ms (full forward)** per side. Linear or **exponential** response modes are available; exponential softens control near zero.
  - The driver API takes direction and power as a percentage for each side.
  - Dimensions and top speed are **not given**. The porter must choose them from Fig. 10.17 and the MMP-5 datasheet; in the photos the chassis is roughly 30 cm.
- **Electronics:** a second Minidragon computes the next waypoint (e.g. physicomimetics) and drives the motors.
- **Obstacle Detection Module (ODM):**
  - Sharp **GP2D12 IR** sensors on a PIC16F767 board. Range **10–80 cm**; up to 8 sensors (power-limited at 33–100 mA each).
  - Output ≈ **2.6 V at 10 cm** (ADC 532) and **0.4 V at 80 cm**, on a 10-bit ADC over 0–5 V. Readings are raw and unfiltered.
  - The response is non-monotonic below 10 cm, so the sensors are mounted at least 10 cm in from the robot edge.
  - The I²C read sequence (slave address 0010 000) returns 16 bytes.
  - Video: `odm.avi`.
- **LCD03** (I²C 0xC6): used for debugging. Example: a Maxelbot **holding a position 26 in directly behind another Maxelbot**, with motor powers near zero at equilibrium (Fig. 10.20).
- **CMPS03 compass** (KMZ51 sensor; I²C 1100 000): bearing as one byte (0–255) or a word (0–3599). Used to align egocentric frames and for global goals; the heading is broadcast in the token packet.
- **AD7415 temperature sensor:** ±0.5 °C, −40 to +125 °C, I²C 1001 000 (Listing 10.4 returns °F). Suggested use: **"a team of robots to follow rising temperatures towards a heat source"**.
- **Bluetooth serial:** up to 7 adapters per PC.
- **Fleet:** **seven Maxelbots** exist (`4robots.avi`, `7robots.avi` outdoor runs). Ch. 11 names A3, U2 and C5.
- **BPP notes:**
  - **New vehicle model needed: skid-steer ground robot.** Use a box chassis with four wheel contacts or a two-track equivalent, with left/right commands in [−1, 1]. The mapping can be linear or exponential (e.g. `cmd^3`, or a sign-preserving exponent; the curve is not specified).
  - Physicomimetics output (a desired displacement vector) must be converted to (v, ω), then to left/right power. A typical scheme is to turn toward the vector, then drive forward; the book does not specify the scheme here (check Ch. 12/13 for the turn-then-move rule).
  - Medium effort. Groundwork for Chs. 11, 12, 14, 15 and the outdoor formations.
  - **IR rangefinder sensor:** BPP raycast, 10–80 cm, with the non-monotonic fold below 10 cm optional. Easy.
  - **Compass:** yaw plus noise, quantized to 1/256 rev. Easy.
  - ★ **Temperature-gradient following** with a heat-source scalar field: a direct sibling of fluxotaxis (Chs. 8–9). It fits in the existing "scalar-field drawing" groundwork and could be a first, simpler field-following demo for ground robots.

## What Ch. 10 adds to / changes in the existing plan
1. **Missing groundwork:** the plan flies everything on quadcopters with omniscient relative positions at 5 Hz. The book's hardware experiments (Chs. 11, 12, 14, 15…) use **Maxelbots**: skid-steer ground robots with egocentric 2D trilateration, a token-ring update of 120 ms per pulse, a range of about 3 m, and IR obstacle sensing at 10–80 cm. A faithful port needs:
   - (a) a **Maxelbot ground-vehicle model**
   - (b) a **trilateration sensor model**, calibrated to 0.6 in mean error at 2 ft and about 11% at 9–11 ft
   - (c) a **token-ring scheduler** that makes neighbour data stale (120 ms × N)
   - (d) an **IR rangefinder**

   These should come before the hardware-replica entries of later chapters.
2. **3D caution:** the real sensor is planar, because of the parabolic cones. A 3D port of any "Maxelbot sensing" chapter needs either a 4-receiver 3D trilateration (★ E10.1) or an elevation-limited sensor. Flag this as a design decision, not an automatic upgrade.
3. **Sensing rate:** the plan's AP at 5 Hz with fresh positions is better than the hardware, where each neighbour is updated at 8.33/N Hz. Running the drone formations with token-ring staleness is a cheap, informative robustness test, and is relevant to Ch. 13.
4. **New small demos:**
   - leader–follower at a 26 in offset (`2robots.avi`)
   - a 3-robot outdoor formation
   - a temperature-gradient (heat-source) follower
   - a sequential "one-robot-moves-per-slot" update mode (E10.11) as a jitter experiment
