## Entries

### 1.1 Swarm success baseline P vs measured Q (Sec. 1.2, PDF 37–38, book pp. 6–7)
- **Shows:** a quantitative test of whether interactions help. With N independent robots, each with success probability p_i, the probability that at least one succeeds is **P = 1 − Π_{i=1..N}(1 − p_i)**. The text calls this the baseline for "minimally interacting" swarms (examples: uniform search in Ch. 11, sentries, surveillance in Ch. 7).
- Measure the success rate of an interacting swarm empirically as **Q**. Q = P means the interactions are neutral, Q < P means they are badly designed, and Q > P means they are well designed. The book's stated aim is Q ≥ P.
- **Agents / params:** generic; no values given.
- **Result to reproduce:** none numeric. It is a metric definition.
- **BPP notes:** this is a batch-run harness feature, not a scene. For any task (goal reaching through obstacles, coverage, intruder catch), run N agents with interactions off (independent) to estimate the p_i, compute P, then run with AP on to get Q. It needs a **batch/Monte-Carlo runner with success logging**, which the Ch. 14/15 work needs anyway. Effort: easy once batch runs exist. ★ Make "Q vs P" the standard summary line for every BPP experiment.

### 1.2 Cooperative pulling/pushing video "pull.avi" (Sec. 1.2, PDF 38, p. 7)
- **Shows:** robots cooperatively pulling an object, as an example of a task that needs interaction. The video only is referenced; the text gives no algorithm, parameters or robot count.
- **BPP notes:** BPP's rigid-body engine suits this well: ground robots or quadcopters with tethers (Bullet constraints) pulling a heavy box. The force law is unspecified, so the porter has to design it (e.g., formation plus goal force applied to the payload). Effort: medium. ★ Multi-quadcopter slung-load carry as a 3D version. Not in the existing plan.

### 1.3 Self-organisation of 40 magnets, "selforg.avi" (Sec. 1.4, Fig. 1.2, PDF 42, p. 11)
- **Shows:** 39 magnets lie on a board, all in the same orientation, so they repel and sit in equilibrium. A 40th magnet dropped from above breaks the equilibrium, and all 40 move, jump and realign into a **linear chain**. There is no global controller.
- **Agents:** 40 physical magnets (dipoles).
- **Result:** qualitative only; the final structure is a line.
- **BPP notes:** you can build this in BPP with rigid cylinders and pairwise **magnetic dipole forces/torques** (orientation-dependent, 1/r⁴ force), with friction on the board. This is the only orientation-dependent (non-central) force in Part I. Effort: medium (needs a dipole–dipole force and torque in Lua). ★ It is a nice demo of self-assembly and a precursor to Ch. 5 docking. You must decide the dipole strength, the friction and the drop height; none are given.

### 1.4 Compass construction of a triangular lattice (Sec. 1.5, Fig. 1.5, PDF 44–45, pp. 13–14)
- **Shows:** a geometric algorithm. Each vehicle sits at the centre of a circle of radius D. Each new vehicle is placed at an intersection of two existing circles. Repeating this builds a triangular (hexagonal) lattice; the figure shows 7 vehicles. The construction is scalable, removing a vehicle does not disturb the rest, and the final shape depends on placement order (footnote 3).
- **BPP notes:** a sequential "place at circle intersection" script that draws the circles is a trivial visual (easy). It serves as the ground truth for the lattice that AP should reach. ★ The 3D analogue is sphere intersections: three spheres meet in 2 points, which gives tetrahedral/FCC packing.

### 1.5 Rubber-sheet / gravity-well model, incremental ("selforg_inc.avi") and batch ("selforg_batch.avi") (Sec. 1.5, Figs. 1.6–1.8, PDF 45–48, pp. 14–17)
- **Shows:** each particle deforms a sheet into a spike at its centre with a circular **trough (potential well) at radius D**. Troughs from different particles add ("constructive interference"), so the deepest wells sit at circle intersections.
  - *Incremental:* 7 particles are added one at a time and each falls into the deepest intersection well (Figs. 1.6, 1.7).
  - *Batch:* all 7 are introduced at once and self-organise into a triangular lattice, but a different one (Fig. 1.8). Newtonian dynamics is deterministic, so the final form depends only on the initial placement (p. 16).
- **Agents:** 7 point particles. The potential's exact form is not given: "details have not been fully fleshed out", and the real force law is in Ch. 3.
- **Result to reproduce:** batch and incremental insertion both converge to a 7-node triangular lattice (one centre plus a hexagon), possibly in different arrangements.
- **BPP notes:**
  - Run a 2D particle replica with the Ch. 3 split-Newtonian or LJ law.
  - Add **scalar-field drawing**: render the summed potential Σ U(r_i) as a height-field mesh under the particles, updated live. This reproduces Figs. 1.6–1.8 directly and is a reusable groundwork item for Ch. 6/8/9 field visualisation.
  - Choose U as the integral of the Ch. 3 force law: split-Newtonian gives a spike and a trough at D; LJ gives a smooth well.
  - Effort: easy–medium.
  - ★ In 3D the "sheet" becomes an isosurface or slice plane of the potential through the drone formation.

### 1.6 MAV hexagonal lattice as a sensing grid / virtual antenna (Sec. 1.4, PDF 40, p. 9, quoting [235])
- **Shows:** the original motivation. MAVs (about US $1000 each) form a hexagonal lattice that acts as a sensing grid or a synthetic-aperture "virtual antenna". In the plume work each robot acts as a CFD grid point (quote from [242]).
- **BPP notes:** this is exactly what the existing quadcopter triangle lattice does. ★ An optional add-on: compute a lattice-quality metric (spacing error, angle error) as a "sensing-grid quality" score.

### 1.7 Principle of least action / least resistance (Sec. 1.4, Figs. 1.3–1.4, PDF 41–44)
- These are photographs of bent steel and flowing water. There is no simulation. The claim worth checking: well-designed AP swarms minimise motion/energy, and are locally (not globally) optimal ("satisficing").
- **BPP notes:** log the total path length and energy (Σ motor power on the drones) per agent as a standard metric, which lets you compare force laws on effort. Easy.

## Chapter roadmap (Sec. 1.6.2, PDF 51–55): facts relevant to the plan

Ch. 1 describes the chapters as follows. These are the points that matter for the port:
- **Ch. 2:** a spring and a simple solar system in NetLogo; conservation of linear/angular momentum and energy.
- **Ch. 3:** self-organising formations, moving to a goal through obstacles, theory for setting parameters, scaling, robustness to loss, **noise improves performance**, and hardware requirements.
- **Ch. 4** has four tasks:
  1. perfect formations (small amount of global information, more communication, violating Newton's 3rd law);
  2. **chain formations**;
  3. **uniform exploration**, with theory used to optimise it;
  4. **"sweeping"** an area with a new **kinetic-theory** AP, with theory that verifies the simulation.
- **Ch. 5:** "local oriented potential fields". Robots that **physically dock** to form one artificial organism; four deployment strategies; behaviours are safe wandering, following, dispersion, aggregation, homing and angularity.
- **Ch. 6:** aquatic sensor networks for bioluminescence. It starts in NetLogo, then a multi-drone simulator, then "water helicopter" vehicles. Applications are ocean monitoring and **submarine stealth**.
- **Ch. 7:** kinetic theory for surveillance and sweeping in obstacle-filled environments. Its theory predicts stochastic behaviour. It is **comparable to a biomimetic method in about 1/3 of the time**.
- **Ch. 8–9:** fluxotaxis for chemical plume tracing. It beats biomimetic methods. On Maxelbots, **3 robots with one chemical sensor each beat 1 robot with 4 sensors**.
- **Ch. 10:** the Maxelbot, with RF + acoustic **trilateration** for range and bearing, obstacle detection, temperature, LCD, compass, and an I²C bus.
- **Ch. 11:** uniform coverage, with a robot-faithful simulator, a proof of correctness, **mean-free-path derivation**, and 3 Maxelbot experiments.
- **Ch. 12:** chain formations, with performance metrics, **sensor and motor noise robustness**, and Maxelbots that report the shape of the environment.
- **Ch. 13:** motion-constrained vehicles. AP sets **waypoints** and a lower-level controller follows them, which matters especially for **fixed-wing (minimum turn rate, stall speed)**. Agents become **multiple particles joined by virtual torsion springs**.
- **Ch. 14:** a distributed learning algorithm (inspired by distributed EAs, and not physics-based) for formations moving to a goal as **obstacle density increases**.
- **Ch. 15:** scouts predict swarm success. There are two formulas, one Bayesian. **Prediction accuracy differs between liquid and solid formations.**
- **Ch. 16:** graph-based design of interaction models. It uses **heterogeneous castes** for **resource protection**.
- **Ch. 17:** a swarm-engineering methodology: a goal equation leads to a "swarm condition", which leads to a model.
- **Ch. 18:** APO, where mass is proportional to fitness. It beats PSO.
- **Ch. 19:** APO that surrounds and **tracks a moving optimum under noise**, using a rigid formation. PSO struggles here. Includes large- vs small-scale landscape structure.

## Contradictions / additions to the existing plan

1. **Ch. 5 (docking / self-assembly, local oriented potential fields) is missing from the plan.** BPP's rigid bodies and constraints suit docking well (★ drones or ground robots that latch together).
2. **Ch. 16 (heterogeneous castes, resource protection, graph-based design) is missing from the plan.**
3. **Ch. 4 covers more than "Perfect Formation".** It also introduces chains, uniform exploration and kinetic-theory sweeping, which are detailed later in Ch. 12, 11 and 7. Those later chapters are in the plan, so Ch. 4 should be ported as the simple first versions of them.
4. **Ch. 13 is more than friction.** Ch. 1 describes it as (a) AP producing waypoints for a lower-level controller, which the drone port already does, and (b) **multi-particle agents with virtual torsion springs** for fixed-wing aircraft. The plan's "friction fixes Ch. 13" needs checking against Ch. 13 itself. The fixed-wing dumbbell is the torsion-spring model.
5. **Ch. 10 trilateration** implies a **sensing model** (range/bearing noise, turn-taking emission, 1-bit or packet piggy-backed on RF) as groundwork for all Maxelbot-faithful ports (Ch. 11, 12). It is not in the plan.
6. **Ch. 15 needs both liquid (LJ) and solid (Newtonian) formations** for its prediction-accuracy finding, so keep both laws available in the scouts port.
7. **Q vs P (entry 1.1)** is a uniform metric worth adding to the batch-run infrastructure.
8. ★ New small demos not in the plan: the magnets chain (1.3), the cooperative pull (1.2), and the potential-field height map (1.5).
