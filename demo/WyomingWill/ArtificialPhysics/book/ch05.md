- Authors: Andrea Bravi, Paolo Corradi, Florian Schlachter, Arianna Menciassi.
- PDF pages 160–175 (book pp. 129–144; book = PDF − 31).
- Context: the EU REPLICATOR project. Robots that physically dock into 3D "multi-robot organisms." The chapter covers the *pre-assembly* deployment phase.

## Core method: LOPF + physicomimetics (§5.2.2, PDF 163–164, Fig 5.1)

**Division of labour.**
- Physicomimetics (split Newtonian, desired distance D) regulates *distance*. It gives a hexagonal lattice by itself (`hexagonal.avi`).
- A **local oriented potential field (LOPF)** regulates the *angle* between robots.

**How an LOPF works.**
- Every robot carries a potential field defined in a frame centred on itself. The frame's axis **j** is common to all robots: global knowledge from a compass, wireless positioning or landmarks.
- The field acts on each neighbour according to that neighbour's bearing relative to **j**, and possibly its distance. The force is added to the physicomimetic force.

**Linear-only fields.**
- Only linear potential fields are used, so the force is constant in magnitude and direction within each angular sector.
- Fig 5.1: 8 sectors labelled α, β, γ, δ around the robot.
- The forces are not equal and opposite, by design: in Fig 5.1, B's force on A differs from A's force on B.

**Hardware requirements.**
- An omnidirectional proximity sensor ring for range and bearing to neighbours.
- A global heading reference.

**Simulated agent model (§5.4, PDF 168).**
- Omnidirectional (holonomic) motion.
- Omnidirectional proximity sensing out to a radius larger than D.
- Knowledge of its own position in a global frame.
- Gaussian noise on both the proximity readings and the coordinates. Sigma not given.
- All runs start from random positions; a **homing** force (a potential toward the formation centre or the arena centre) draws robots in.

## Entries

### 5.1 Square deployment via LOPF
§5.3.1, PDF 164–165, Fig 5.2. Snapshots in Fig 5.6, PDF 169.

**Field (from Fig 5.2).**
- Space around the robot is split into 8 equal 45° slices. The slice boundaries are the diagonals and the **j**/⊥ axes.
- Each slice applies the same constant-magnitude force, directed perpendicular to the nearest axis and *toward* it.
- Effect: a neighbour is pushed onto the N, S, E or W axis, so the robots end up in a square lattice.

**Why it is needed.** The physicomimetic square lattice needs Ch.3 "virtual colour" (desired distance D or √2·D), plus balanced colour counts, a dynamic colour assignment and communication. The LOPF gives a square lattice with none of these, and for any number of agents.

**Result to reproduce.** Robots home to the centre and form a square lattice. The text claims scalability: the pattern adapts as agents join (Fig 5.7 caption).

### 5.2 Linear deployment
PDF 165, Fig 5.3. Snapshots in Fig 5.7.

- Same as square, but only the slices that push neighbours toward the **j** axis are kept ("avoiding two sets of parallel forces"; each quadrant split into 2 slices).
- Neighbours are pulled onto the line along **j**, giving a line or snake.
- Uses: snakelike 3D organisms, patrolling, moving through dangerous places.

### 5.3 Star deployment (leader + arms)
PDF 166, Fig 5.4. Snapshots in Fig 5.8, PDF 170.

**Roles.**
- One **leader** (black) is unaffected by any LOPF. It is known to all, by a visual marker or a broadcast.
- Its LOPF ("basic star"; three arms in the figure: one along **j** and two at angles α, β below) pushes its neighbours onto the arm rays.
- All other robots carry a *linear* LOPF oriented along their arm, which extends the arms.

**Join rule.** A robot joining the formation is affected only by the LOPFs of robots already deployed. This stops LOPF and physicomimetic forces from fighting over angles, and the authors found it more stable. It is optional; a larger LOPF gain would also work.

**Levels.**
- Red = the leader's neighbours, which feel only the leader's LOPF.
- Orange = the rest of the deployed robots.
- Level assignment uses only the distance and angle to the leader (no communication). The thresholds are hand-tuned, which the authors call "considerable effort."

**Any number of arms is possible**, given the physical robot size.

### 5.4 Star "gripper": actuated arms
Fig 5.11, PDF 171.

- Rotate the **j** axis of the linear LOPFs on two of the three arms by +γ and −γ. Those two arms close together like tweezers or a gripping hand.
- One parameter (γ) drives the motion.
- Uses: a hand-organism that moves objects, or the start configuration for spiderlike forms.

### 5.5 Tree (triangle / V) deployment
PDF 167, Fig 5.5. Snapshots in Fig 5.9, PDF 170; level assignment in Fig 5.10, PDF 171.

**Shape.** The leader sits at the tip of the triangle.

**LOPFs (Fig 5.5).**
- One LOPF for the leader and the robots down the middle.
- One for robots on the west edge (**j** = north).
- One for robots on the east edge.
- The angles α set the width of the triangle.

**Rules.**
1. A robot feels the LOPF and physicomimetic forces only of robots at *lower* levels (level 2 ← level 1; level 3 ← levels 2 and 1; and so on).
2. Robots on the same level repel each other.
3. Before it has a level, a robot interacts with all deployed robots through their LOPFs.

**Level assignment (Fig 5.10).**
- A robot at the proper angle and in the range [d1, d2] from the leader is level 1; in [d2, d3] it is level 2; and so on (multiples of D).
- Fig 5.10 example: 7 robots, 3 levels. Fig 5.5 example: 6 robots.
- Colours: red, orange, yellow by level.
- Red robots interact only with the leader's LOPF, orange only with red robots', yellow only with orange robots'.

**Uses.** Compact moving formation, a battering ram, the torso of an organism. Moving the leader drags the whole formation.

**Inspiration.** The "angularity" behaviour is motivated by barnacle geese V-formations (PDF 168).

### 5.6 Tree shape morphing
Fig 5.12, PDF 171.
- Changing α at run time changes the triangle's aperture; the formation becomes more compact.

### 5.7 Behavioural-coordination basis set mapped to physicomimetics
§5.2.1 and §5.3.2, PDF 162–163 and 167–168. Snapshots in Figs 5.13–5.15, PDF 172.

Matarić's basis behaviours, plus a new one (angularity), each expressed with physicomimetics or LOPF:

| Behaviour | Implementation |
|---|---|
| Safe wandering | Physicomimetic repulsion only, inside D (attraction switched off) |
| Following | Keep a neighbour within an angle, at distance D (physicomimetics) |
| Dispersion | Safe wandering with the repulsion radius extended to the maximum (sensor range) |
| Aggregation | Attraction without the sign switch below D. Fig 5.13: D changed mid-run, agents re-space |
| Homing | A potential field toward a home location. Fig 5.15: home = arena centre, leader in black |
| **Angularity** (new) | Fixed relative bearing between robots, emerging from the LOPF |

Composites suggested in the text:
- Flocking = homing + dispersion + aggregation + safe wandering.
- Foraging = homing + dispersion + safe wandering + following.
- Surround an object = aggregation toward the object + inter-agent dispersion.
- Homogeneous field coverage = aggregation, then dispersion at the maximum radius.
- Behaviours are binary-switchable (1/0), which suits online learning or evolution. The text suggests evolving the physicomimetic, LOPF and behaviour parameters as a genome (cf. Daedalus, Ch.14).

**Whole-formation rotation.** Rotating **j** rotates the whole deployment while preserving the relative positions. Proposed use: object transport.

### 5.8 Holes and the choice of starting configuration
PDF 171–173.

- Problem: holes, where a robot is not correctly deployed because of a bad initial position or because the others block it.
- Remedies discussed:
  1. Motion noise, which needs delicate tuning.
  2. Extra approach rules, which add complexity.
  3. Start from an easier formation. A hexagonal physicomimetic lattice is best (isotropic, compact, 6 neighbours); square is less compact (4 neighbours).
- No measurements are given.

### 5.9 Sensorial property: "angle of attention"
§5.5.2, PDF 174. Analysis only, no simulation.

- In a line of square robots with 4 cameras each, about half of each robot's view is occluded by its neighbours (except at the tips), so that half of the sensor data can be discarded. This saves CPU and battery.
- Checkable in BPP by ray-casting occlusion fractions per formation.

## BPP conversion notes

**Groundwork.**
- A **sector-based anisotropic force term** in a global frame: neighbour bearing relative to **j** → slice index → constant force vector. This is a simple Lua function added to the existing physicomimetic force.
- A global heading (yaw or compass) for every agent. Drones have this.
- Leader/level bookkeeping, with level thresholds as parameters.
- A homing potential.
- Gaussian sensor and position noise.
- Holonomic agents. Quadcopters are effectively holonomic in the horizontal plane, which suits this chapter better than wheeled robots.

**Particle replica first (2D), effort easy–medium.**
- Square, line, star, tree, plus the behaviour library (aggregation with changing D, dispersion, homing, safe wandering).
- **The porter must choose:**
  - LOPF magnitude relative to FMAX;
  - D, G and p (use the Ch.3 defaults);
  - noise sigmas;
  - the level thresholds d1, d2, d3 (≈ k·D ± tolerance);
  - the angular tolerance for "proper angle";
  - α for the tree;
  - arm angles for the star (e.g. 120° apart);
  - homing gain.
- The slice geometry should be checked against Figs 5.2–5.5 (book PDF pages 165–167).

**Metrics to add (the book gives none).**
- Bearing error to the nearest lattice axis.
- Number of holes or defects.
- Time to formation.
- Behaviour as N grows (scalability).
- For the gripper: arm angle vs. γ.

**Quadcopters (fixed altitude), effort medium.**
- Same law at 5 Hz via waypoints.
- The LOPF forces are piecewise constant and discontinuous at slice boundaries. Expect chattering at the boundaries on drones; smoothing the sectors (e.g. a force ∝ sin of the angular error) is a decision for the porter.

**★ 3D ideas the chapter invites.** The chapter's whole aim is 3D organisms; its simulations are 2D.
- *3D LOPF*: two global axes (**j** = north plus **k** = up, from compass + gravity). Sector partition on the sphere, e.g. push neighbours onto the 6 ±x/±y/±z axes for a **cubic lattice** (a 3D square deployment without virtual colours).
- *3D star*: a leader with 4 tetrahedral or 6 octahedral arms; "spider" organisms.
- *3D tree / V*: a cone, or a flying V wedge of drones (the geese analogy). α sets the wedge angle; morph it in flight (Fig 5.12 analogue).
- *Gripper in 3D*: drone arms closing around a floating or ground object (★ a cooperative "grasp" demo in BPP with a rigid-body payload).
- *Rotating j*: rotates the whole formation rigidly. Demonstrate a formation-wide yaw rotation.
- *Docking*: BPP is a rigid-body simulator, so a final phase could actually dock ground modules (hinge or fixed constraints when aligned). That is the REPLICATOR goal and goes beyond the book.

**Ambiguities.**
- Whether LOPF forces depend on distance (Fig 5.1 says constant per quadrant) and their range: presumably the sensor range.
- Whether the leader also exerts physicomimetic forces: yes for distance, it seems; it just doesn't receive any.
- The exact homing target: the barycentre "calculated with respect to the position of the leader" (Figs 5.8, 5.9).

## Additions / contradictions to the existing plan
- **LOPF is absent from the plan.** It is a cheap addition, reusing the formation code plus a global-frame sector force, and gives four new formations (square without colours, line, star, tree/V) plus actuated formations (gripper, V-morph, rigid rotation).
- The tree/V deployment overlaps the plan's "flocking" item. A V-formation of drones is a natural 3D showcase.
- The square deployment via LOPF is an alternative to the Ch.3 virtual-colour square lattice the user already has. It is worth comparing the two directly (defects vs. N).
- The suggestion to evolve the LOPF and behaviour parameters links to the Daedalus/learning-loop item (Ch.14).
- Caveat: no quantitative benchmarks exist, so the "results to reproduce" are only qualitative. The port defines its own metrics.
