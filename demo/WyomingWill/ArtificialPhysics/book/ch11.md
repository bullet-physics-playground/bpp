# Chapter 11 — Uniform Coverage

- **Author:** Paul M. Maxim
- **PDF pages:** 372–397 (book pp. 341–366)
- **Summary:** a single robot (and, in theory, N independent robots) achieves **uniform coverage** of any connected environment that can be tiled with square cells, whether convex or concave. It does this with a random-turn-at-walls rule plus a **mean free path** (mfp) random turn of length about 0.6 × cell size.
  - Uniformity is measured with the KL divergence. It is justified with a Markov chain argument (an ergodic chain with a symmetric transition matrix has a uniform steady state).
  - It is validated with a C/Freeglut simulator and three Maxelbot hardware experiments.
- **Model names:**
  - NetLogo model: **`uniform_mfpl.nlogo`**, multi-robot (summary, PDF 397). It is introduced in Ch. 4.3, which gives the mfp upper bound 17⅓ for its arena; that is someone else's chapter.
  - Video: `serial.avi`.
- Natural log is used in KL. I verified this: recomputing from the listings and tables reproduces the printed values exactly with ln.

## E11.1 Maxim's uniform-coverage simulator (C + Freeglut 2.4.0)
- **Section/pages:** §11.2–11.2.2, PDF 373–375, Fig. 11.2.
- **Agent:** one robot, drawn as a disk with a heading dot. It has a forward obstacle sensor drawn as a short line.
- **Parameters:**
  - From the Fig. 11.2 screen header: **sensor range 17.0 px**, **response range 1.0 px**, display speed 4 ms per step.
  - The response range is the distance at which the robot reacts to a wall. It is set to **1.0** so that the periphery is not neglected (Gage's condition).
- **Run protocol:** **10 million time steps**. Per-cell visit counts are written every **10,000 steps**. Start position is varied between a corner, side or interior cell; the final distribution is reported as independent of start.
- **Not stated:** robot speed per step (presumably 1 px/step; the porter must decide), robot radius, and turning dynamics (turns are instantaneous in the sim).
- **Ambiguity:** "visits" means **time steps spent in the cell** (occupancy), not entries. Ch. 11.6 says the same for hardware: readings inside the cell perimeter.
- **BPP notes:**

    - forward-only wall sensing
    - stop when a wall is within the response range

    - mfp counter

  - Easy.

## E11.3 KL-divergence metric
- **Section/pages:** §11.2.3, PDF 375–376, Eq. 11.1.
- `D_KL(P‖Q) = Σ_i P(i) ln(P(i)/Q(i))`, where P is the observed occupancy fraction and Q = 1/C is uniform.
- **Targets:** **0.001** in simulation; about **0.01** for real robots. 0.01 counts as "very good".
- **BPP notes:**
  - Implement it as a shared Lua utility, logged as a time series and drawn as a per-cell grayscale heat map (Figs. 11.5, 11.10, 11.16, 11.21).
  - Easy. Reused by the Ch. 4/7 coverage work.

## E11.4 Idealized Markov chain (existence proof)
- **Section/pages:** §11.2.4, PDF 376–377.
- **Model:** the robot sits at a cell centre, picks one of four directions uniformly, and either stays (a wall edge) or moves to that neighbour's centre.
  - A cell with b peripheral edges stays put with probability b/4. Diagonal moves are ignored.
  - The 9×9 Q for the 3×3 grid is printed on PDF 377: corner self-loop 0.50, side 0.25, centre 0.
- **Result:** Q is symmetric and ergodic, so `lim Qᵗ` gives **1/9 per cell**. I verified numerically that Q²⁰⁰ gives 0.1111 in every entry.
- **Principle:** any correct algorithm must give an ergodic, **symmetric** Q. The algorithm is complete if the environment is connected.
- **BPP notes:**
  - Pure theory, but a good **diagnostic**. In a BPP run, count cell-to-cell transitions to estimate Q̂ and report its asymmetry ‖Q̂ − Q̂ᵀ‖. That makes "symmetry ⇒ uniformity" visible.
  - Easy. ★ The same diagnostic generalizes to cubic cells in 3D, with 6 neighbours and self-loop b/6.

## E11.5 First algorithm: random turn at walls only (negative control)
- **Section/pages:** §11.2.5–11.2.6, PDF 378–379, Listings 11.1–11.2.
- **Rule:** move forward. If an object is directly in front (within the response range), stop. Pick a random turn, and keep turning randomly until the front is clear, then move forward.
- **Results to reproduce:**
  - **4 cells, 40 × 40, cell 20, 10 M steps** (Listing 11.1): 2,503,625 / 2,500,184 / 2,494,401 / 2,501,790. This is near-uniform because all cells are corners; I compute KL ≈ 1×10⁻⁶.
  - **9 cells, 60 × 60** (Listing 11.2): not uniform. Corners ≈ 1.23 M (0: 1,242,516; 2: 1,229,541; 6: 1,225,855; 8: 1,228,890). Sides ≈ 1.05 M (1: 1,048,036; 3: 1,042,531; 5: 1,052,790; 7: 1,041,632). **Centre: 888,209**, the lowest, because the robot passes straight through it.
  - The book gives no KL for this case; I compute ≈ **0.0057** (ln).
- **BPP notes:**
  - It must reproduce the ordering **corner > side > centre**. This is the key "physics fixes it" contrast with E11.6.
  - Easy; same code with mfp = ∞.

## E11.6 Second algorithm: random turn at walls + mean-free-path turns
- **Section/pages:** §11.2.7, PDF 379–381, Listing 11.3, Fig. 11.3.
- **Rule:** as in E11.5, but the robot also makes a random turn after travelling a distance **mfp = f × c**, where c is the cell size. Empirically **f ≈ 0.6** works best; f = 0.60 is used.
  - **Ambiguity:** the text does not say whether the travelled-distance counter resets after a wall-induced turn. Resetting it is the natural reading (the "distance between successive collisions"). It also does not say whether the random turn is a uniform heading in [0, 360°).
- **Results to reproduce (9-cell, 60 × 60, c = 20, 10 M steps):**
  - Counts 1,098,245; 1,110,451; 1,112,045; 1,124,196; **1,127,813 (centre)**; 1,124,090; 1,089,300; 1,111,144; 1,102,716.
  - **KL = 0.000060.** I verified this with ln.
  - **Fig. 11.3** (KL every 10k steps): it starts noisy at about 0.002–0.008, has a spike of about 0.005 near 0.3 M steps, falls below about 0.001 by about 1.5–2 M steps, and is about 0 by 3 M steps. It is a single run.
- **Ergodicity note:** the time average equals the ensemble average, so one long run is enough.
- **BPP notes:**
  - Easy, and is the main target.
  - ★ Sweep f ∈ [0.2, 1.5] and plot the final KL, which should be minimal near 0.6. Also show convergence time against f: a smaller mfp is more uniform but slower to spread.

## E11.7 Derivation of the mean free path (closed form + Monte Carlo)
- **Section/pages:** §11.3, PDF 380–383, Fig. 11.4, Listing 11.4, Eqs. 11.2–11.4.
- **Definition:** the mfp is the mean distance from a uniform point (x1, y1) in a unit cell to a uniform point (1+ε, y2) on the shared edge of the adjacent cell.
- **Values:**
  - Monte Carlo with x2 = 1.001 and N = 10⁷ gives **0.6517**.
  - The integral `∫∫∫ sqrt((1−x1)² + (y1−y2)²)` gives **0.651757** (antiderivative F printed, thanks to Mathematica).
  - With a 5% wall buffer (response range 1 on cell 20), the theory gives **0.6079**, matching the empirical f = 0.60. I reproduced 0.6079 with x1 ∈ [0.05, 1] and y1, y2 ∈ [0.05, 0.95].
- **Claim:** the mfp makes Q symmetric, because it is the same average distance into any adjacent cell, whether that cell is on the periphery or not.
- **BPP notes:**
  - Trivial to check in Lua.
  - ★ **3D analogue (for the plan's "coverage 3D"):** for cubic cells, the mean distance from a uniform point in a unit cube to a uniform point on an adjacent face is **≈ 0.7717** (my Monte Carlo, 4 M samples). With a 5% buffer on each side it is **≈ 0.714**. So the 3D rule should use **f ≈ 0.71–0.77 × cube edge**, not 0.6. Verify empirically with a sweep, as Maxim did.

## E11.9 Multi-robot theory (independent robots)
- **Section/pages:** §11.5, PDF 384.
- **Formula:** with C cells and N non-interacting robots, P(a given cell has at least one robot) = `1 − (1 − 1/C)^N` (Eq. 11.5). Choose N so that this exceeds P_m (Eq. 11.6). The text calls N = 10–50 realistic.
- **Examples (mine):**
  - C = 84 needs N = 58 / 193 / 385 for P_m = 0.5 / 0.9 / 0.99.
  - C = 20 needs N = 14 / 45 / 90.
- **Multi-robot simulation:** NetLogo `uniform_mfpl.nlogo` (PDF 397).
- **BPP notes:**
  - ★ This is a closed-form, checkable prediction. Run N particles (with or without mutual avoidance), record per-step occupancy of each cell, and compare the fraction of time each cell is occupied with Eq. 11.5.
  - Inter-robot avoidance (which the theory ignores) is a measurable deviation.
  - Easy–medium.

## E11.10 Hardware method: one moving Maxelbot, stationary Maxelbots as "wall" localizers
- **Section/pages:** §11.6, PDF 384–388.
- **Unit mapping:** 1 sim px = **1 inch**; 1 sim step = **1 second**.
  - Cells are **2.5 ft × 2.5 ft (30 in)**, so mfp ≈ 0.6 × 30 = **18 in**. (The text gives no explicit hardware mfp; this is implied.)
- **Moving robot:** a Maxelbot (Ch. 10) whose ODM uses **two IR sensors at the front corners, aimed straight ahead** (GP2D12, 10–80 cm). It emits an RF + acoustic pulse every **120 ms (8.33 Hz)**; 100 readings ≈ 12 s.
- **Monitors:** stationary Maxelbots embedded in the walls trilaterate the mover and stream positions over serial to a PC.
  - Occupancy is the number of trilateration readings inside each virtual cell, with fixed corner coordinates.
  - Readings outside the arena are discarded for analysis.
- **Walls:** pine beams **15 ft × 6 in × 10 in**, on 12 in floor tiles. One wall in the square arena is a 5 ft table top.
- **BPP notes:**
  - Needs a **Maxelbot ground-robot model** with 2 forward IR raycasts (see ch10.md E10.13).
  - The "stationary monitor" is simply the trilateration sensor model (E10.1/E10.9) applied from fixed points, with range-dependent noise.
  - Medium. ★ The monitor noise model can be validated against the impossible out-of-arena points seen in Fig. 11.14.

## What Ch. 11 adds to / changes in the existing plan
1. **The plan says "coverage 3D (Ch. 11)", and the existing particle coverage is likely based on Ch. 4.** Ch. 11 adds several pieces the port should include first, in 2D:
   - the cell/KL machinery (logging every 10k steps, a heat map)
   - the **first algorithm as a negative control** (corner > side > centre in 3×3)
   - **f = 0.6 × cell**, with the theory value 0.6517 and 0.6079 with a buffer
   - the 12-environment × 5-size table (geometries must be reconstructed)
   - the multi-robot formula, Eq. 11.5
2. **3D coverage specifics** (my derivation, not in the book):
   - Use cubic cells and a symmetric transition structure with 6 neighbours.
   - The mfp is ≈ **0.77 × edge** (≈ 0.71 with a 5% buffer) rather than 0.6.
   - Random turns should be **uniform on the sphere**.
   - Walls, floor and ceiling are sensed by a forward rangefinder.
   - For quadcopters, a "random turn" is a new velocity direction (yaw is irrelevant). The response range must exceed the braking distance, otherwise the periphery buffer, and hence f, change. Expect the empirical f to differ from theory; sweep it.
   - ★ Concave 3D environments (an L of boxes, multi-room buildings, stacked floors connected by a shaft) are the natural showcase, since Gage says non-convex coverage is hard.
3. **A hardware-replica track is missing from the plan.** Building it needs ground-robot Maxelbot coverage with:
   - 2 forward IR sensors
   - monitors with trilateration noise and merging
   - 1 px = 1 in and 1 step = 1 s
   - an 8.33 Hz reading rate

   Ch. 10's vehicle and sensor groundwork is a prerequisite.
4. **★ Multi-robot coverage with drones:** Eq. 11.5 gives a direct design rule for "how many drones to keep at least one in every room/volume cell with probability P_m". Validate it in BPP with N quadcopters (independent, or with simple avoidance).
