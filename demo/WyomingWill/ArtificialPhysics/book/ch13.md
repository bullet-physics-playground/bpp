# Chapter 13 — Physicomimetic Motion Control of Physically Constrained Agents

- **Authors:** Thomas B. Apker and Mitchell A. Potter (NRL)
- **PDF pages:** 444–471 (book pp. 413–437; book page = PDF page − 31). Equations were checked against rasterised pages 447, 448, 454, 458, 462–465 and 467.
- **Why it matters for BPP:** Ch. 13 explains the quadcopter Newtonian jitter. It is the immediate next build: adaptive friction (13.23), adaptive speed cap (13.24), static friction (13.25), the torque law (13.15–13.18) and the dumbbell agent (13.19–13.22).
- **No NetLogo models** are named in this chapter. Experiments are on Pioneer 3-AT hardware (from ref [263]) and in an unnamed fixed-wing simulation.

**Main idea:** Physicomimetics is an *outer-loop guidance* law. It outputs a desired velocity, and an inner-loop controller and vehicle must achieve it (Fig. 13.1). Point-mass physicomimetics asks for things real vehicles cannot do. Four factors determine a vehicle's **reachable space**, the set of (tangential speed u_t, heading change ΔΨ = Ω·Δt) it can achieve in one outer-loop step (§13.2.1, p. 449):
1. Controller time resolution
2. Ability to change heading
3. Maximum speed and acceleration
4. Minimum speed and acceleration

## E1. Reachable space of a point-mass particle
**§13.2, p. 447, Fig. 13.2.**

- **Settings:** Δt = 0.1 s, u_t,max = 1.0 m/s, maximum heading change π.
- **Result:** The reachable space is the full rectangle, ΔΨ ∈ [−π, π] × u_t ∈ [−0.5, 0.5] m/s as plotted. The caption says 1.0 m/s but the axis spans ±0.5; this is an inconsistency in the book.
- **Meaning:** With no turn-rate or acceleration limit, a particle can jump to any velocity below u_max in one step.
- **BPP:** A plotting utility. Sample commands from the controller and scatter them over the reachable-space envelope. Useful as a diagnostic for every vehicle model. Easy.

## E2. Split Newtonian law, first-order forward Euler (FOFE) update, and the discontinuity at D
**pp. 447–448, Eqs. 13.1–13.3, Fig. 13.3.**

- **Eq. 13.1 (as printed):**
  - F_ij = −G·(m_i m_j)^{p_m} / r_ij^{p_d} if r_ij < D
  - F_ij = +G·(m_i m_j)^{p_m} / r_ij^{p_d} if D ≤ r_ij ≤ D_max
  - F_ij = 0 otherwise
- **Sign conflict:** The prose says the force is "positive (repulsive) if r < D, negative (attractive)" beyond. The equation has the opposite signs. Fig. 13.3 plots repulsion as positive. **Use the prose and figure convention: positive = repulsive.**
- **The five parameters:** G, mass power p_m, distance power p_d, attract–repel (AR) boundary D, and range D_max.
- **Fig. 13.3 values:** D = 1 m, D_max = 4 m.
  - Force is +200 at r = 0.5, +100 at r → 1⁻, jumps to −100 at r → 1⁺, about −25 at r = 4, then 0.
  - These values fit **G·(m_i m_j)^{p_m} = 100 with p_d = 1**, i.e. 1/r. The caption calls it an "inverse square rule". For a reproduction of the figure, use 100/r.
- **FOFE update:**
  - v_{n+1} = v_n + Δv, with Δv = (ΣF/m_p)·Δt (13.2)
  - If ‖Δv‖ ≫ v_max: v_{n+1} = (ΣF/‖ΣF‖)·v_max (13.3)
  - In other words, large forces reduce the controller to "full speed in the direction of the force".
- **Key claim:** The sharp sign flip at D lets a point mass dither across D with net displacement near zero. Physical systems cannot do that.
- **"Jumpiness" near the AR boundary** scales with Δt·v_max (§13.2.1.1, p. 449). With multiple agents, commands computed for one configuration keep being applied after the agents have moved. The swarm then oscillates about "low energy" configurations. Asynchronous agents cannot meet the Nyquist criterion, so **each agent needs internal damping logic**.
- **This is the book's explanation of the BPP quadcopter jitter:**
  - AP runs at 5 Hz, a large Δt.
  - Waypoint following adds lag.
  - The sign flip at D does the rest.
- **BPP:** Already implemented as the Ch. 3 Newtonian law. Add a Fig. 13.3/13.8 force-curve plot as a unit test. Easy.

## E3. Differential-drive reachable space
**§13.2.1.2, pp. 449–451, Eqs. 13.4–13.6, Figs. 13.4–13.6.**

- **Robots:** Pioneer P3-DX and Talon (photos). Fig. 13.5 shows that the particle location (e.g. the laser) is **not at the rotation centre**.
- **Kinematics:**
  - u_t = (v_r + v_l)/2 (13.4)
  - Ω = (v_r − v_l)/(2·r_diff) (13.5)
  - u_t,max = v_max − Ω·r_diff (13.6)
- **Fig. 13.6 settings:** Δt = 0.1 s, v_max = 0.5 m/s, r_diff = 0.3 m.
  - The reachable space is a **diamond**: u_t = ±0.5 at ΔΨ = 0, falling linearly to 0 at ΔΨ = ±v_max·Δt/r_diff = ±0.167 rad.
- **Reference:** Ellis & Wiegand [58] reduced forward speed during large heading changes with a "friction" term. This improved settling time for a simple lattice (§13.2.1.3, p. 452).
- **Aircraft and boats:** Stress grows with v², so the allowable turn rate depends on speed.
- **BPP:**
  - A ground-robot vehicle model (two wheels, skid steer) or a point mass with a unicycle limit.
  - Make the particle offset from the rotation centre a parameter, because it is a known failure source.
  - Effort: medium (new vehicle).

## E4. Pioneer 3-AT reachable space with a minimum wheel speed
**§13.2.1.4, pp. 452–453, Fig. 13.7.**

- **Settings:** r_diff = 0.3 m, Δt = 0.1 s, |v_wheel| ∈ [0.1, 0.5] m/s (or 0).
- **Result:** The diamond of Fig. 13.6 with a **gap** around zero speed and zero rotation. The figure shows two half-diamonds above and below a central small diamond, separated by white lines. Small position changes are impossible.
- **Also listed:** Fixed-wing minimum turn rate comes from the smallest holdable bank angle and minimum speed from stall. DAC resolution makes actuation discrete.
- **Consequences:**
  - The minimum step sets the achievable lattice quality.
  - Agents get stuck in "low-energy" formations because the forces are too small to move them.
- **BPP:** Add a deadband or minimum-command option to the vehicle models. Quadcopters effectively have one too: waypoint tolerance and position-hold noise. Easy.

## E5. Point-mass control of a wheeled robot, with the AR damping factor η
**§13.2.2, pp. 453–454, Eqs. 13.7–13.10, Fig. 13.8; results from ref [263].**

- **Speed and turn-rate update (μ multiplies the whole bracket):**
  - u_t,new = (u_t,old + ΣF·t̂/m_p)·μ (13.7)
  - Ω_new = (Ω_old + ΣF·n̂/m_p)·μ (13.8)
  - Here t̂ is the tangential (forward) unit vector, n̂ the normal along the axle, and μ the "friction factor".
  - **Note:** In this older formulation μ is a retention factor (multiplier), not 1 − friction.
- **Consequence:** A force along n̂ produces zero instantaneous displacement. This adds lag that the guidance design did not account for.
- **Damping factor (controller at 10 Hz):**
  - η = (‖D − r_ij‖ / D)^{p_d} if r_ij < 2D (13.9)
  - F_used = F·η (13.10)
  - For r ≥ 2D (up to D_max), η is presumably 1. The text gives no value; check against Fig. 13.8, which is consistent with η = 1 beyond 2D.
- **Fig. 13.8 (D = 1, D_max = 4, same 100/r base as E2):**
  - r = 0.5: η = 0.5, F = +100
  - r = 1: F = 0, smooth through the boundary
  - r = 2: minimum ≈ −45 to −50
  - r = 4: ≈ −25, then 0
- **Behaviour:** Robots slow as they approach D. The outer loop sees ‖ΣF‖ fall below a **preset threshold (value not given) and stops the robot**. With these two heuristics, the Pioneers settled into fixed lattices resembling simulation.
- **Drawbacks:** No orientation control (cannot point cameras), and the "triangle of death" (E6).
- **BPP:**
  - η is a two-line change to the existing AP law and is the **cheapest first jitter fix for quadcopters**. Apply η for r < 2D, then a stop threshold on ‖ΣF‖ (hold position).
  - Porter decides the threshold, e.g. a fraction of the force at r = 1.5D.
  - Effort: easy.
  - **Done (M1 step 1):** `lib/ch13.lua`, sliders `etaPower` and `stopForce`
    in `quadcopter/swarm.lua`. Measured: η alone settles the Newtonian drones
    like LJ; with range 1.5 D in 2D the lattice settles at ~0.8 D (range
    1.25 D keeps D); power 1 works better than the law's 2.2; the stop rule
    only helps with η (`quadcopter/README.md`, FINDINGS F43–F47).

## E6. "Triangle of death"
**pp. 454–456, Figs. 13.9–13.11.**

- **Setup:** Three differential-drive robots on an equilateral triangle, with **axles pointing at the centre**.
- **What happens:** Each robot sees almost no force along the direction it can move (t̂). The surrounding forces turn it back to its zero-movement orientation. An extra attracting force parallel to the axle (Fig. 13.11) makes the robot rotate slightly.
  - If the particle is at the rotation centre, the robot turns to face the attractor and drives off, dragging the others along.
  - If the particle is offset from the rotation centre, rotating moves the particle into an opposing turning force. The robot stops or oscillates in minimum-speed rotations forever and needs manual intervention.
- **Settings:** No numbers given beyond the geometry. Force fields are plotted from robot 3's view.
- **BPP:**
  - A reproduction test for the ground-robot model. Use E5's law, D = 1, place the three robots, add an attractor, and compare particle at the centre vs. offset (e.g. 0.2 m forward).
  - Expected: escape vs. lock or oscillation.
  - Not relevant to holonomic quadcopters. It is a good demonstration of why the dumbbell model (E10) is needed.
  - Effort: easy once E3 exists.

## E7. Fixed-wing UAV reachable space
**§13.2.3, pp. 457–458, Eqs. 13.11–13.14, Fig. 13.12.**

- **Assumptions:**
  - The inner loop holds speed and altitude and limits bank angle Φ_max ≈ 20°.
  - Lift L = mg.
  - Inner loop ≥ 50 Hz, outer (physicomimetic) loop **10 Hz**.
  - Bank and speed changes are treated as instantaneous.
- **Equations:**
  - F_n = L·sin Φ (13.11)
  - a_n = (L/m)·sin Φ = g·sin Φ (13.12)
  - Δv = [0, Δt·g·sin Φ]ᵀ in (tangential, normal) components (13.13)
  - **Ω_max = g·sin(Φ_max)/(2v)** (13.14)
- **Example:** v = **13 m/s**, Φ_max = **20°**.
  - The reachable space is a short horizontal segment at u_t = 13 m/s. Fig. 13.12 shows ΔΨ of about ±0.13 rad.
  - My computation: Ω_max = 9.81·0.342/26 = **0.129 rad/s**, so the minimum turn radius is v/Ω ≈ **101 m**. This matches the roughly 100 m loops in Fig. 13.16. Note: Eq. 13.14's factor of 2 is unexplained; a standard coordinated turn, v²/(g·tanΦ), gives about 47 m. The figure's loops support the book's value, so model Eq. 13.14 and state the choice.
  - Note: the true coordinated turn gives g·tan Φ/v = 0.275 rad/s (radius ≈ 47 m). Eq. 13.14 is the book's (conservative) choice; use it to match the figures.
- **Conclusion:** The UAV cannot stop and cannot reverse instantly, so it cannot "lose energy". **Point-mass physicomimetics cannot be used for fixed-wing formation control.**
- **BPP:**
  - A fixed-wing kinematic model (constant speed, turn rate clipped at Ω_max, optionally first-order bank lag).
  - Effort: easy as a kinematic "point-mass with limits". A full 6-DOF fixed-wing is hard (the book mentions the Dutch-roll mode, p. 466).

## E8. Single UAV attracted to a single point
**pp. 458–459, Fig. 13.13.**

- **Setup:** Point-mass force law. Only Eq. 13.8 (turning) has any effect, because speed is constant.
- **Three outcomes:**
  1. Attractor **directly behind**: the normal component is 0 and the UAV flies away forever.
  2. Attractor outside the two **unreachable zones**: the UAV overflies it in a continuous loop.
  3. Attractor inside an unreachable zone: the UAV orbits forever at the closest reachable distance and never reaches it.
  - The unreachable zones are the two minimum-turn-radius circles tangent to the UAV at its sides.
- **Also:** With an attractor, the orbit radius is set by forward speed, not by D.
- **BPP:** A good unit test for the fixed-wing model with the gravity law. Place the attractor at the three positions and check for the three behaviours. Easy.

## E9. Heading-particle torque law
**§13.3, pp. 460–462, Eqs. 13.15–13.18, Fig. 13.14.**

- **Idea:** Replace the point mass with a body that has mass and rotational inertia, and add **virtual torsion springs** between agents and points.
- **Torque law:**
  - Attractor: **τ_ij = −κ·θ_ij if r_ij ≤ D_max, else 0** (13.15)
  - Repulser: τ_ij = +κ·θ_ij
  - θ_ij is the angle between the agent's heading and the bearing to particle j (Fig. 13.14), and κ is the spring constant.
- **Co-heading law (form not given):** Applies a torque toward a neighbour's heading or a compass heading, which gives boids-like alignment.
- **Turn-rate update:**
  - τ = Σ_i τ_ij (13.16)
  - ΔΩ = (τ/I)·Δt (13.17)
  - Ω_new = Ω_old + ΔΩ (13.18)
- **Claim:** For constant-speed agents this gives direct access to the only controllable state. The point-mass law degenerates to a **bang–bang** directional controller.
- **Not given:** values of κ, I and D_max. For the UAV case, κ is "set so that no single particle pair interaction will cause a command that exceeds the maximum turn rate": κ·π·Δt/I ≤ Ω_max for the worst case θ = π. Porter computes κ from this.
- **Sign convention:** θ_ij should be wrapped to (−π, π]. With θ measured counter-clockwise from heading to bearing, an attractor needs Ω toward θ, i.e. the torque has the sign of θ. The −κθ in the book is therefore relative to their convention, in which θ is the heading error. **Porter must choose the sign so that attractors turn the agent toward them.**

## E10. Dumbbell (multi-particle) agent
**§13.4, pp. 462–463, Eqs. 13.19–13.22, Fig. 13.15.**

- **Geometry:** Two particles, p_front at distance r_front ahead of the rotation centre and p_back at r_back behind it, both on the heading axis. Values are not given. The front particle's heading is the vehicle heading, so torque laws act on it as well as force laws.
- **Equations:**
  - F_agent = ΣF_pf + ΣF_pb (13.19)
  - **M_agent = T_applied + r_pf × ΣF_pf + r_pb × ΣF_pb** (13.20)
    - T_applied is the sum of torque-law torques (Eq. 13.16).
    - r_pf and r_pb are vectors from the rotation centre to each particle.
    - In 2D, the cross product is r_x·F_y − r_y·F_x.
  - **u_new = (u_old + ΣF·t̂/m)·(1 − μ_ut)** (13.21)
  - **Ω_new = (Ω_old + M·I⁻¹)·(1 − μ_Ω)** (13.22)
- **Notes:**
  - There is **no explicit Δt** in 13.21 and 13.22; it is absorbed into m and I. Contrast Eq. 13.17, which has Δt.
  - Friction is **subtractive** here: (1 − μ) multiplies the whole updated value, so μ = 0 means no damping and μ = 1 means full stop.
  - There are two independent "knobs", μ_ut and μ_Ω.
  - Only the tangential component of force drives u. The normal component acts only through the moment arm (13.20).
- **BPP:** This is the core new agent model, a few lines of code. Effort: easy to medium.
  - For a ground robot: u and Ω go to the wheels via Eqs. 13.4–13.5.
  - For a quadcopter (holonomic, but with yaw that matters for a camera): u becomes the forward velocity command in body frame, Ω the yaw rate. Optionally add a separate lateral channel, or use the dumbbell only for yaw and pointing.
  - For a fixed-wing: u is fixed; use only 13.22 with Ω clipped to Ω_max (a single heading particle suffices; see E13 step 3).

## E11. Pioneer 3-AT dumbbell controller
**§13.4.1, pp. 463–465, Eqs. 13.23–13.25. This is the main recipe to port.**

Hardware: Pioneer 3-AT, reachable space as in Fig. 13.7 (r_diff 0.3 m, wheels 0.1–0.5 m/s). Outer loop **10 Hz**. Laser or sonar ranging.

### (a) Outer-loop time resolution: adaptive friction (anti-oscillation)
- At 10 Hz the vehicle keeps moving toward obstacles for up to 0.1 s too long, and the Nyquist criterion cannot be met. Fixed damping hurts reactivity, so the friction is learned with **two integrators, one for μ_ut and one for μ_Ω**.
- **Eq. 13.23:**
  - **μ_new = μ_old + k_more·(1 − μ_old)** if u_new·u_old < 0 (the command changed sign)
  - **μ_new = μ_old − k_less·μ_old** if u_new·u_old ≥ 0 (same sign, or zero)
- Apply this separately to μ_ut using u, and to μ_Ω using Ω.
- μ stays in [0, 1] automatically when 0 < k ≤ 1. It grows toward 1 when the command oscillates and decays geometrically toward 0 otherwise.
- **Requirement for the Pioneer: k_more/k_less > 10.0** to stop oscillations between mutually attracted robots.
- **Absolute values are not given.** Suggestion: start with k_less = 0.01–0.02 and k_more = 0.2 at 10 Hz (ratio 10–20), then scale to the BPP 5 Hz loop.
- **Order of evaluation (ambiguous):** Compute the tentative u_new with the old μ via 13.21, then update μ by comparing the sign with u_old. Porter decides whether the new μ applies this step or next; next step is the simplest and causal.

### (b) Changing heading: mass-to-inertia ratio
- **m : I = 1 : 1** in 13.21 and 13.22 gave good performance. The ratio should favour moving in the tangential direction quickly.
- **Mission:** **3–5 Pioneer 3-ATs form an oriented ring around a single attractor, with front cameras pointing inward.** Best if robots arrive at their slots already in the correct orientation, because fine heading changes are easier while moving than when stopped (minimum-speed gap).
- **Emergent behaviour to reproduce:** If the attractor is directly behind, the robot does an automatic **three-point turn**.
- **Not given:** ring force-law parameters (D, G, κ), r_front and r_back, and how "pointed inward" is encoded. Presumably an attractor acts on p_front via the torque law, plus robot–robot repulsion. Porter designs this.

### (c) Maximum speed and acceleration
- **Caps:** u_new and Ω_new are always clipped to **u_max = 0.5 m/s** and **Ω_max = 0.4 rad/s**.
- **Adaptive speed cap (Eq. 13.24), applied for each laser or sonar range reading r_m:**
  - **u_max,new = u_max,old + k_more·(0.5 − u_max,old)** if r_m > 1.0 m
  - **u_max,new = u_max,old − k_less·(u_max,old − 0.1)** if r_m ≤ 1.0 m
  - The cap grows toward 0.5 m/s with no object nearby and shrinks toward 0.1 m/s as more objects are within 1 m.
  - **Here the ratio is reversed: k_less/k_more > 10.0** prevented collisions while still allowing fast motion in the open.
  - These k's are separate gains from (a).
- **Note:** Because the update runs once per reading, the number of beams strongly affects the rate. With N beams, roughly one reading in N needs to be close for a net shrink to happen. Porter must pick a beam count, or normalise by it.

### (d) Minimum speed and acceleration: static friction
- **Problem:** Adaptive friction removes most jitter, but poor position estimates can cause long-term minimum-speed oscillation.
- **Eq. 13.25 (as printed):**
  - u_new = (u_old + ΣF·t̂/m)·(1 − μ_ut) if **μ_ut·m·g < ΣF·t̂·m**
  - u_new = 0.0 otherwise
- **Interpretation:** μ_ut acts like a Coulomb static-friction coefficient. When the robot has been oscillating, μ_ut is high and a much larger force is needed to move at all. A large force then means the environment really changed.
- **Printed form has issues:**
  - The right-hand side "ΣF·t̂·m" is dimensionally odd: force × mass versus force.
  - It has no absolute value, so a backward force always fails the test.
  - **Recommended implementation: μ_ut·m·g < |ΣF·t̂|.** If the porter keeps the printed form, use |ΣF·t̂|·m and note that it equals the recommended form when m = 1.
  - The text says the check applies to "applied forces and moments", but only the u-equation is given. The analogous rotational check is presumably μ_Ω·I·g' < |M|, with g' chosen by the porter.
- **g:** 9.81 m/s² as written, which means μ_ut·m·g is in force units of the virtual law. With virtual forces of order 100 (Fig. 13.3 scale), μ·g ≈ 0–9.8 is a sensible threshold range.
- **Result to reproduce:** With 13.23 + 13.25, the Pioneers **settle into fixed positions in two or three passes across their mutual AR boundaries** and stay put until a new force is applied.

### BPP notes for E11 (the immediate build)
- **Where to apply:** In the AP controller of each quadcopter, after computing ΣF at 5 Hz:
  1. Damp the force with η (Eq. 13.9) if desired.
  2. Compute tentative velocity commands with the (1 − μ) factor: either per axis (x, y, z for a holonomic quad) or u/Ω for a dumbbell.
  3. Update μ per axis with Eq. 13.23.
  4. Apply static friction (Eq. 13.25) per axis or on ‖F‖.
  5. Clip to the caps, with the cap adapted by Eq. 13.24 using range to the nearest neighbour or obstacle.
  6. Waypoint = position + v·Δt, or send the velocity directly if a velocity-command interface exists.
- **Groundwork:** A velocity-command interface to the PX4-style controller is better than waypoints, which add lag that was not modelled.
- **Metrics to log:**
  - number of AR-boundary crossings until settling (target 2–3)
  - residual RMS velocity after settling
  - the μ traces
  - the lattice-quality metrics already in the Ch. 3 port
- **Experiments to run:**
  - Newtonian triangle lattice on drones: baseline vs. η vs. adaptive μ vs. adaptive μ + static friction.
  - LJ for comparison.
  - A two-drone "mutually attracted" test to show the effect of the k_more/k_less > 10 ratio.
- **Effort:** easy to medium for quadcopters as particles with friction. Medium for a full dumbbell ground robot with the 3–5 robot camera ring.
- ★ **3D:** Per-axis μ (x, y, z) handles altitude oscillation, which is often worse on quadcopters because of thrust lag. The dumbbell yaw torque law allows camera-pointing rings, i.e. drones orbiting or holding around a point of interest with cameras inward.

## E12. Fixed-wing surveillance: gravity law vs. torque law
**§13.4.2, pp. 465–467, Fig. 13.16.**

- **Setup (simulation):**
  - **5 planes, 10 targets** placed randomly in a small central rectangle, roughly 60 m × 80 m around the origin in Fig. 13.16; the world plot spans ±400 m.
  - Targets are attractors **with no AR boundary**: they always attract, via either the inverse-square law or the torque law.
  - Agents repel each other with the same type of law.
  - Speed 13 m/s and Φ_max = 20° are implied by §13.2.3; 10 Hz outer loop.
  - Torque-law κ is set so that no single pair interaction commands more than Ω_max.
  - Planes enter from outside: the traces start around (−250, −380) and (−420, 180).
- **Results to reproduce (one run each):**
  - **Gravity (point-mass) law, top plot:** Disorganised motion. Agents almost always turn at the maximum rate chasing a changing force vector, and they spread over a wider area, with loops of about 100 m radius scattered up to about ±250 m.
  - **Torque law, bottom plot:** The swarm quickly settles into **overlapping loops that pass over the target area once per orbit**. Time over target is higher and turning (energy) lower. Loops are clustered in about ±200 m.
- **Metrics to compute (the book reports none):**
  - fraction of time within the target rectangle
  - mean |Ω|
  - spread (RMS distance from the target centroid)
- **Not given:** targets' spatial distribution, κ, I, G, D_max, repulsion range, start positions, run length, and whether targets are consumed. Reading the figure, targets persist.
- **BPP:**
  - Requires the fixed-wing kinematic model from E7, which is new. A target-attractor point set, a trail plot, and a comparison script are also needed.
  - Effort: medium.
  - ★ **3D:** Add altitude-separated layers, or a flight-path-angle torque (the book notes 3D needs a second angle, p. 446), for fixed-wing surveillance over a ground target.

## E13. Design procedure (conclusion)
**§13.5, pp. 466–468.**

1. Model the vehicle with its inner-loop controller.
2. Determine the reachable space in commanded (u_t, ΔΨ).
3. Build an agent with "heading particles": one particle for a vehicle that can only turn (fixed-wing), and particles in front of and behind the rotation centre for a vehicle that goes forward and backward (ground robot).
4. Use a friction model to stabilise each agent. Friction has little effect on formation shape but strongly affects oscillation and obstacle avoidance, and it can self-tune when the undesired behaviour is rigorously described.

- **Note:** Discarding the holonomic point mass breaks some analysis tools. More complex agent models, as in molecular dynamics (e.g. water), may be needed.
- **BPP:** Use this as the checklist for every new vehicle in the project: quadcopter, ground robot, ASV, AUV, fixed-wing.

## Contradictions and additions to the existing plan

- **"Friction fixes Ch. 13" understates the chapter.** Ch. 13 gives four separate mechanisms, and they should be built and ablated in order:
  1. AR-boundary damping η (13.9–13.10) with a stop-on-small-force threshold.
  2. **Adaptive** oscillation friction μ (13.23), per channel, with k_more/k_less > 10.
  3. **Static** friction gate (13.25), which needs a fix to its printed condition.
  4. Adaptive speed cap near obstacles (13.24), with k_less/k_more > 10.

  Plain constant friction (Ch. 3 style) is not what fixes the problem.
- **The Newtonian vs. LJ observation is explained (§13.2.1.1, §13.2):**
  - The split Newtonian law has a sign discontinuity at D. Combined with finite Δt (5 Hz), lag, and asynchronous neighbours, it produces oscillation about the AR boundary.
  - LJ is smooth through zero force, which is effectively what η does.
  - Prediction for the BPP drones: **η alone should already make Newtonian drones behave like LJ.**
- **New vehicle models the plan does not list:**
  - differential-drive ground robot (Pioneer 3-AT: r_diff 0.3 m, wheels 0.1–0.5 m/s, u_max 0.5 m/s, Ω_max 0.4 rad/s, 10 Hz)
  - constant-speed fixed-wing (13 m/s, Φ_max 20°, Ω_max = g·sin Φ_max/(2v))
