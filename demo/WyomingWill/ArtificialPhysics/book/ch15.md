# Chapter 15 — A Statistical Framework for Estimating the Success Rate of Liquid-Mimetic Swarms

- **Authors:** Diana F. Spears, Richard Anderson-Sprecher, Aleksey Kletsov, Antons Rebguns
- **PDF pages:** 506–535 (book pp. 475–503). Book page = PDF page − 31.
- **Running header title:** "Scouts for Estimating Swarm Success Rate".
- **Core content:**
  - Send k ≪ n expendable scouts through an obstacle course and measure their success fraction p̂.
  - Predict P(Y ≥ y), the probability that at least y of the n swarm agents reach the goal, with a Bernoulli formula or a new Bayesian (Beta-prior) formula.
  - Evaluate the predictions by MSE against simulated ground truth.
- The swarm uses the Ch. 14 evolved Lennard-Jones (LJ) law. There is a side experiment with split-Newtonian.
- **No NetLogo model** is used. The simulator is the authors' own 2D simulator ("Robotic Swarm Simulation", Fig. 15.1).

## Entry 15.1 — Bernoulli prediction formula P_Bern (§15.2.2, §15.3.2, PDF pp. 511–515)

**What it is:** closed-form theory that a simulation can check.
- p̂ = k_success / k.
- P(Y = y | p̂) = C(n,y) p̂^y (1−p̂)^(n−y) (Eq. 15.6).
- **P_Bern = Σ_{j=y..n} C(n,j) p̂^j (1−p̂)^(n−j)** (Eq. 15.7). This is the binomial upper tail.
- It assumes independent trials, which inter-agent forces violate (p. 518).
- The authors say it is valid when k is large, "optimally more than 20".

**BPP conversion:** pure math, a few lines of Lua. Use the log-gamma form for n up to 500.

## Entry 15.2 — Bayesian prediction formula P_Bayes (§15.3.3, PDF pp. 515–517)

**Formula:**

P_Bayes = ∫₀¹ ∫₀ᵖ M · z^(y−1) (1−z)^(n−y) · p^(r−1) (1−p)^(s−1) dz dp, where:
- M = [B(r,s) · B(y, n−y+1)]⁻¹;
- **r = k·p̂ + α**;
- **s = k(1−p̂) + β**;
- B is the Beta function.

**How to read it:**
- The prior is Beta(α, β) on p.
- The inner integral is the binomial upper tail written as a regularized incomplete beta, I_p(y, n−y+1) = P(Bin(n,p) ≥ y).
- The outer integral averages over the Beta(r, s) posterior.
- It is **not** the same as plugging the posterior mean into P_Bern (footnote 3).
- It is in fact the beta-binomial upper tail: P_Bayes = Σ_{j≥y} C(n,j) B(j+r, n−j+s) / B(r,s). That closed form is easier to implement than the paper's n-point Gaussian quadrature (p. 517). The derivation is in [189].
- With a uniform prior (α = β = 1) and large k, P_Bayes ≈ P_Bern.

**Priors used (§15.5.1, PDF p. 521):**

| Prior | α | β |
|---|---|---|
| uniform | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| weak, p ≈ 0.1 | 2.1 | 18.9 |
| strong, p ≈ 0.1 | 10.9 | 98.1 |
| weak, p ≈ 0.5 | 2.5 | 2.5 |
| strong, p ≈ 0.5 | 14.5 | 14.5 |
| weak, p ≈ 0.9 | 18.9 | 2.1 |
| strong, p ≈ 0.9 | 98.1 | 10.9 |

- Prior "strength" = 1/variance.
- "Correctness" = |prior mean − true p|.

**BPP conversion:** pure math in Lua: a log-Beta via lgamma plus a sum. Easy.

## Entry 15.3 — Swarm navigation simulator (§15.4, Fig. 15.1, PDF pp. 517–519)

**Agents:** point agents with physicomimetic LJ control. They sense range and bearing to:
- obstacles and other agents within **1.5D**, with **D = 50 pixels**;
- the goal, at any range.

The force law is the Ch. 14 generalized LJ: F_ij = 24ε(dδ^12/r^13 − cδ^6/r^7) with D = 2^(1/6)δ.

**Parameters from the Fig. 15.1 panel** (read at 300 dpi):
- Agent # 40, Obstacle # 90, Timer Delay 1, **Max Velocity 1.20**.
- Strength ε: A–A 6.75, A–O 8.99, A–G 10.72.
- Attraction c: A–A 1.43, A–O 4.45, A–G 5.25.
- Repulsion d: A–A 2.29, A–O 9.38, A–G 5.19.
- Force cutoff (Fmax): A–A 1.08, A–O 4.32, A–G 1.87.
- Friction is not shown.
- Max Velocity 1.20 is per time step, presumably. Ch. 14 says Vmax = 20 units/s.

**World:**
- Area of **800 × 600 pixels**.
- Agents start uniformly at random in a **100 × 100 starting square** in the lower-left corner.
- The goal (a square) is in the upper-right corner, about **1,000 pixels** from the start. The diagonal of 800 × 600 is exactly 1000.
- Obstacles are large circles. The screenshot shows about 90 of them, with a radius of roughly 10 pixels judging by the image.
- The run ends at **t = 1,500 time steps**.
- Success = reaching the goal by the end of the run. The arrival radius is not given here; Ch. 14 uses 4D.
- The screen draws lines between agents within sensing range (connectivity).

**Modes:** independent agents (no inter-agent forces) or LJ-coupled. This chapter only reports the coupled mode. Earlier work [189] showed excellent accuracy with independent agents, as theory predicts.

**BPP conversion:** this is the same world as Ch. 14's offline environment, rescaled. Build it once as a 2D particle world with the three LJ laws and reuse it for both chapters. Easy to medium.

## Entry 15.4 — Experimental methodology: hand-crafted difficulty, ground truth, MSE (§15.5–15.6, PDF pp. 519–524)

**Environments:**
- Three **hand-crafted** obstacle layouts give a true single-agent success probability **p = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9**. The goal location and the number, size, shape and position of obstacles were tuned until p came out right; the layouts are not given.
- p is measured from **1,000 runs** of the n-agent simulation.
- The figures actually report p = 0.55 and 0.51, so the achieved p differs slightly from the target.

**Factorial design:**
- n ∈ {100, 200, 300, 400, 500}.
- k ∈ {3…15}.
- y from 1 to n.
- p ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 0.9}.
- 7 priors.

**Ground truth:** P_Truth = the fraction of **1,000 runs** in which at least y of the n agents reach the goal. The start positions are re-randomized each run.

**Prediction procedure:**
1. Run the k-scout simulation **100 times** and average to get E[p̂].
2. Plug E[p̂] into P_Bern and P_Bayes.

**Metric:** MSE = E[(P_B − P_Truth)²] = Bias² + Variance, with E taken over 1,000 runs.
- When y is not on the x-axis, curves are averaged over all y.
- The text is internally inconsistent: E[p̂] is obtained from 100 runs, but the MSE is "averaged over 1,000 runs".

**Scouts and swarm:** both use LJ, and the scouts are not part of the n agents.

**BPP conversion:** this needs a **headless batch mode**: hundreds of thousands of runs of up to 500 agents for 1,500 steps. Particle-only, no rigid bodies, for speed.
- Effort is hard mainly because of compute and the need to design the obstacle layouts.
- Practical simplification: reproduce one cell first, for example n = 100, k = 5, p ≈ 0.55 (Fig. 15.2).

## Entry 15.5 — Hypothesis 1: prior correctness and strength (Fig. 15.2, PDF pp. 524–525)

**Claim, confirmed:**
- A correct prior is better than an incorrect one.
- If the prior is incorrect, a weak one is better, and uniform beats any incorrect prior.
- If the prior is correct, a strong one is better than a weak one.

**Fig. 15.2:** p = 0.55, n = 100, k = 5. MSE is plotted against y = 0…100. Values below are read from the plot.

| Curve | Shape and peak |
|---|---|
| MSE(P_Bern) | about 0.25 for y ≲ 15, falling steeply to about 0.05 near y ≈ 30, with small bumps until about y = 80 |
| P_Bayes, uniform prior | bimodal, peak ≈ 0.06 at y ≈ 25–30, second hump ≈ 0.02 at y ≈ 70 |
| P_Bayes, weak almost-correct prior | peak ≈ 0.03 |
| P_Bayes, strong almost-correct prior | lowest, ≲ 0.02 |

The curves are "double-humped" (bimodal); the reason is explained in [189].

**BPP conversion:** this follows from Entries 15.2 and 15.4. It is the best first target.

## Entry 15.6 — Hypothesis 2 (more scouts helps?), 2.1, 2.2 (Fig. 15.3, PDF pp. 525–527)

**H2, "more scouts improve accuracy":** **mixed or violated.**
- Fig. 15.3: p = 0.51, n = 300, k = 3…15. Values read from the plot:
  - MSE(P_Bern) ≈ 0.10 at k = 3, 0.065 at k = 5, 0.095 at k = 10, 0.075 at k = 15;
  - MSE(P_Bayes, uniform) ≈ 0.02–0.045 throughout.
- **Why:** under LJ forces the scouts move as a quasi-unit, as the swarm does. The effective sample size is about 1, so adding scouts barely helps in easy or moderate environments.
- In hard environments (p near 0) the formation fractures, and more scouts do help.

**H2.1 (confirmed):** in easy or moderate environments, a few scouts are predictive whatever the swarm size.

**H2.2 (confirmed):** in hard environments, k must scale in proportion to n.

**BPP conversion:**
- The quasi-unit effect is a direct, testable emergent property.
- ★ Measure the fragmentation of scouts and swarm, for example the number of connected components at the end, against p. It is cheap to log.

## Entry 15.7 — Hypothesis 3: scalability at constant k/n = 0.03 (Figs. 15.4–15.6, PDF pp. 527–529)

**Setup:** uniform prior for P_Bayes. The x-axis is k/n = 3/100, 6/200, 9/300, 12/400, 15/500. Values below are read from the plots.

| Environment | MSE(P_Bern) by k/n | MSE(P_Bayes) by k/n | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|
| **p = 0.9** (Fig. 15.4) | 0.14, 0.125, 0.035, 0.07, 0.095 | 0.08, 0.07, 0.025, 0.05, 0.06 | Falls from 100 to 300 agents, rises toward 500, but 500 is still below 100. |
| **p = 0.5** (Fig. 15.5) | 0.10, 0.065, 0.095, 0.097, 0.107 | 0.03, 0.02, 0.045, 0.063, 0.063 | Slight rise only. |

- **Confirmed:** scaling n with k/n held fixed does not increase the error.
- P_Bayes beats P_Bern except at p = 0.1.

## Entry 15.8 — Hypothesis 4: robustness to environmental difficulty (Figs. 15.7–15.8, PDF pp. 529–531)

**Fig. 15.7 (n = 100, k = 5), the first trend.** The x-axis is p (environmental difficulty); values are read from the plot.

| Curve | p = 0.1 | p = 0.5 | p = 0.9 |
|---|---|---|---|
| MSE(P_Bern) | 0.02 | 0.065 | 0.085 |
| P_Bayes, uniform | 0.033 | 0.025 | 0.05 |
| P_Bayes, strong correct | ≈ 0.003 | ≈ 0.003 | ≈ 0.003 |

Text: P_Bayes never goes "significantly above 0.05", while P_Bern → 0.09 as p → 0.9.

**Fig. 15.8 (n = 300, k = 10), the second trend.** Values are read from the plot.

| Curve | p = 0.1 | p = 0.5 | p = 0.9 |
|---|---|---|---|
| MSE(P_Bern) | 0.01 | 0.095 | 0.035 |
| P_Bayes, uniform | 0.015 | 0.045 | 0.025 |
| P_Bayes, strong correct | 0.005 | 0.035 | 0.008 |

**Conclusions:**
- **P_Bayes is robust; P_Bern is not.**
- At p = 0.1, uniform-prior Bayes is worse than Bern.
- A strong correct prior is always best.

**Overall conclusions (§15.8, PDF pp. 531–532):**
- Both formulas are accurate.
- Both scale when k grows in proportion to n.
- Bayes is robust and usually better with fewer than 20 scouts.

## Entry 15.9 — Liquid (LJ) vs solid (split-Newtonian) scouts (§15.9, PDF p. 532)

**What it shows:** the framework was re-run with the **split-Newtonian** law.
- Predictive accuracy was **considerably worse** than with LJ.
- **Why:** under split-Newtonian, small groups (the scouts) squeeze through narrow gaps, but a large solid-like swarm gets stuck. Scouts therefore do not represent the swarm.
- Under LJ, a small blob of liquid flows like a big one, so scouts do represent the swarm.

**No numbers are given and the Newtonian parameters are not stated.** Reuse the Ch. 3 split-Newtonian law with G, p and Fmax as in the existing BPP port.

**BPP conversion:** medium, given Entry 15.4.
- ★ This is the most physically interesting result, and it maps onto the drones well: compare the scout-vs-swarm success gap for LJ drones and for Newtonian drones in a gap course.
- Note that Newtonian jitters on drones (Ch. 13), so this comparison could be confounded on drones. Do it on particles first.

## Entry 15.10 — Porting to real robots (§15.10, PDF p. 533)

- The noise effect is equivalent to a "harder" environment.
- The key assumption is that scouts are **typical** of the swarm: the same hardware and the same noise, chosen at random from the pool.
- ★ For BPP drones this is directly testable. Give drones per-unit variations (motor lag, mass, sensor noise) and check whether random scouts still predict the swarm.

## Practical scenario, with no experiment (§15.11, PDF p. 534)

1. The scouts' success rate suggests that fewer than half the swarm will succeed.
2. The operator later gets intelligence that the environment is easy.
3. He updates the prior and recomputes P_Bayes without sending more scouts, and may now decide to deploy.

This could make a nice interactive BPP demo: a prior slider that recomputes P(Y ≥ y) live.

## Contradictions and additions to the existing plan

1. **"Scouts (Ch. 15)" depends on Ch. 14's world.**
   - Ch. 15 uses the Ch. 14 three-law LJ obstacle course; Fig. 15.1 gives the only explicit evolved parameter values in the book.
   - Build the 2D LJ goal-plus-obstacle world once, as a particle model, and port Ch. 14 offline and Ch. 15 from it.
   - Ch. 15 is mostly **statistics plus batch runs**, not new physics.
2. **The main cost is compute.**
   - Ground truth needs 1,000 runs per (n, environment), with n up to 500 for 1,500 steps, plus 100 scout runs per k.
   - BPP needs a headless, no-render, particle-only batch mode with results written to CSV. This is not in the plan and is shared groundwork with Ch. 14, Ch. 11 coverage and others.
   - Drones are impractical for the full factorial; use them only for a showcase cell.
3. **Hand-crafted environments with p = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9 must be designed by the porter.** The layouts are not given, so tune them by search: for example, vary the number or size of obstacles until 1,000 runs give the target p.
4. **Add the split-Newtonian comparison (Entry 15.9).** It ties to the existing Ch. 3 Newtonian code and gives a crisp "liquid beats solid for prediction" result.
5. ★ **3D idea:** scouts as drones over a 3D obstacle field, such as a column forest or "urban canyon". Scouts that climb over obstacles while a large formation must flow around them would break the "scouts are typical" assumption. That is worth demonstrating.
